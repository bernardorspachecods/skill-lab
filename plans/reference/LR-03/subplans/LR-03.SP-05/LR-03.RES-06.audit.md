# Audit

## Question and scope

How should an LLM structure a web-research deliverable for its intended
consumer, and when should the answer, confidence, sources, and detailed audit
trail be presented separately? The consumer and stakes are not specified in
the brief. I treat the requested example—practices grouped by topic or
situation, with source and confidence details available separately—as a
representative use case, not a universal template. This audit seeks evidence
about usability, traceability, and trust; it does not establish one best layout
for all audiences or tasks.

## Claims and evidence

- **C1 — Supported with limits.** Structure should follow the requested use
  and task. Evidence from a benchmark and a qualitative user study supports
  task-sensitive organization and sufficient context, but does not compare
  alternative layouts for ordinary research summaries.
- **C2 — Supported with limits.** A direct answer or concise summary can be
  distinct from detailed methods and evidence records while preserving
  traceability. Keep enough support and material uncertainty in the answer;
  separation alone does not ensure usability or auditability.
- **C3 — Supported with limits.** Citations and uncertainty cues should be
  accessible and claim-relevant, but they are not neutral trust indicators.
  Citation presence and confident language can increase perceived reliability
  without establishing correctness. The evidence supports calibration and
  checkable links, not a fixed placement or confidence scale.

### Findings

For the representative request, a defensible design is to group findings by
the user's topics or situations, state each practical finding directly, and
keep its material qualification and supporting citation close enough to assess
it. A concise answer can link to a more detailed evidence record containing
the provenance and validation needed for audit. The exact hierarchy and how
much detail to expose depend on the task and reader. This is a workflow
judgment inferred from evidence below, not a layout tested as a complete
package.

The evidence does not support hiding all uncertainty or sources in an
appendix. The consumer-facing answer should preserve qualifications that could
change how the finding is used, express confidence in relation to the evidence
rather than rhetorical certainty, and offer an obvious route to claim-level
sources. Separate records are useful for reproducibility and deeper checking,
but more links or longer explanations do not by themselves make the answer
more correct or trustworthy.

### Evidence entries

- **E1: C1, C2 -> S1 (EFSA uncertainty reporting guidance, “Where to report”
  and “Prepare reporting for the summary section”).** The page says the
  location should maximize transparency and accessibility, and names separate
  identifiable sections as one option (lines 32–33). It calls for a concise
  uncertainty summary suitable for the executive summary in addition to
  detailed methods and results (lines 42–44). This directly supports
  summary-plus-detail as an acceptable reporting pattern and requires
  uncertainty to remain legible in the summary. It is authoritative guidance
  for scientific assessments, particularly EFSA assessments; it does not
  experimentally establish that answer-first improves usability in general
  web research.
- **E2: C1, C2, C3 -> S2 (Venkit et al., FAccT 2025, pp. 3–5, findings and
  Table 1).** In a qualitative
  study, 21 expert users compared three answer engines with Google on technical
  and opinion/debate queries. The paper reports concerns about insufficient
  contextual detail (21/21), missing citations (18/21), overconfident wording
  (16/21), misattributed citations (21/21), and sources listed without being
  used in the answer (13/21). Table 1 separately groups issues under answer
  text, citations, sources, and interface (p. 3). This supports keeping direct
  answers sufficiently contextualized, integrating citations with claims,
  avoiding unused-source displays, and making the source path intelligible.
  It also cautions that excessive compression can make findings superficial.
  Its small, self-selected expert sample and existing answer-engine interfaces
  do not establish preferences of general consumers or test the proposed
  summary-plus-audit pattern.
- **E3: C3 -> S3 (Ding et al., AAAI 2025, pp. 2–4, methods/results).** A
  randomized between-subjects
  experiment presented ChatGPT answers of at most three sentences to 303
  Prolific participants, varying citation count (zero, one, five) and
  validity; it tracked mouse checks and self-rated trust across ten questions.
  Citation presence increased perceived trust; five citations did not
  significantly outperform one. Only 193 of 1,976 cited answers were checked.
  The paper's analyses distinguish valid from random citations: random
  citations were rated less trustworthy in one comparison, while the abstract
  summarizes a trust increase with citations even when random. This nuance
  means the study is not evidence that invalid sources are harmless. It does
  show that citation presence and count are poor substitutes for verifying
  whether sources support claims. The short answers, generic open questions,
  and self-reported trust limit generalization to sustained research reports.
- **E4: C3 -> S4 (Li and Aral, 2025 preprint, abstract and results §§3–4,
  lines 4–7, 86–94, 109–113).** A preregistered randomized
  experiment with a U.S.-representative sample (n=4,927) tested generative
  search design choices. The authors report that citations/reference links
  increased trust and willingness to share even when some links were
  incorrect or hallucinated; confidence highlighting reduced trust and
  willingness to share. Participants reporting greater trust clicked more
  and spent less time evaluating results. This is direct evidence that such
  interface cues can move trust and checking behavior without validating
  correctness. It is a 2025 arXiv preprint (not a peer-reviewed publication
  located here); stimuli and nine topic-specific search tasks limit
  transfer to other research outputs and audiences.
- **E5: C3 -> S5 (Steyvers et al., Nature Machine Intelligence 2025; preprint
  abstract lines 3–6).** In multiple-choice and short-answer tasks,
  participants tended to overestimate LLM answer accuracy when shown default
  explanations; longer explanations increased confidence without increasing
  accuracy. Calibrating explanation language to model confidence narrowed
  measured human-model calibration and discrimination gaps. This supports
  concise, evidence-calibrated uncertainty and warns against treating length
  as reassurance. The task is not web research, and model confidence is not
  identical to evidence quality or source confidence; direct transfer is
  limited.
- **E6: C1, C3 -> S6 (Du et al., ICLR 2026 conference paper, §§3.1–3.2,
  4.2–4.3, Appendix C).** The final paper retains the report-quality RACE
  framework and separate FACT framework for effective citation count and
  citation accuracy. RACE uses task-specific weights/criteria across
  comprehensiveness, insight/depth, instruction following, and readability.
  The benchmark retains 100 expert-designed tasks across 22 fields. Its human
  consistency study uses 50 Chinese tasks, reports from four agents, and
  70+ master's-level domain-expert annotators (three annotators per task).
  The rubric describes readability through clear structure/ease of
  understanding and instruction following through direct task response. This
  supports assessing answer/report quality and citation support as related
  but distinct dimensions, with criteria adapted to a task. The final paper
  explicitly treats FACT as a complementary observational dimension: some
  systems' citations could not be parsed, and crawler access/content
  differences affected measurements; the benchmark's overall rankings rely
  on RACE. This is an evaluation benchmark, not a user-preference study or
  validation of a consumer-facing report layout. Its human consistency study
  covers only the Chinese subset and expert annotators, so it does not
  establish a format preference for general consumers or other languages.

## Conflicts, limitations, and refutation

- Shorter is not always better: the FAccT participants reported missing
  contextual detail and support when answer-engine summaries were too generic.
- Citations can invite trust without corresponding verification. The AAAI
  controlled task observed infrequent source checks; Li and Aral's larger
  AI-search experiment reports that reference links increased trust and
  sharing even when links were incorrect, and that uncertainty highlighting
  reduced trust. These findings concern trust/reliance outcomes,
  not whether disclosure is intrinsically undesirable; they reinforce the need
  for accurate claim-source links and calibrated explanations.
- The benchmark separates readability/task fit from factual citation support,
  but does not establish that physical separation of the consumer answer and
  audit is optimal. EFSA explicitly allows a separate uncertainty section
  while requiring a concise summary. Together, these sources argue for
  accessible layering with material caveats retained in the top-level answer,
  rather than a universal separate appendix.
- No located study directly compares (a) topic/situation-organized practical
  recommendations with confidence/sources linked separately against (b) a
  fully integrated answer, across representative consumers and research tasks.
  The requested example is therefore a reasonable design target, not an
  empirically selected universal format.

## Workflow judgment and recommendation

The evidence supports these implementation choices as judgments, not direct
experimental findings:

1. Infer the output structure from the user's intended use. For a request for
   practices, organize by the named topic or situation when that grouping
   helps retrieval and action.
2. Lead with direct findings. Include enough rationale and context to
   interpret each finding, plus any caveat that could change its use.
3. Place sources next to the claims they support or provide an unambiguous
   claim-level path to them. A bibliography of merely retrieved sources is not
   an evidence trail.
4. Make confidence meaningful and calibrated, explaining material uncertainty
   rather than relying on an unexplained numeric score or confident tone.
5. Put detailed provenance, extraction, conflicts, and validation in a linked
   evidence record when audit needs justify it. Keep disposable search notes
   separate; do not force all process details into the answer.

## Verification and stopping

- Opened primary/authoritative records for EFSA reporting guidance, the FAccT
  2025 user study, the AAAI 2025 citation experiment, Li and Aral's 2025 AI
  search preprint, the Nature Machine Intelligence uncertainty study record,
  and the final DeepResearch Bench ICLR 2026 paper.
- Verified S6's final venue/version and author list against its published
  OpenReview paper and ICLR 2026 proceedings listing; checked E6's framework,
  task, human-evaluation, rubric, and limitation statements against the final
  paper text. The core E6 findings remain supported; the citation-measurement
  limitation is more explicit in the final version than in arXiv v1.
- Checked study tasks, participant counts, paper dates/versions, relevant
  result passages, and scope limitations against source pages/PDF text; did not
  rely on search snippets as evidence.
- Checked the contradictory-seeming AAAI abstract and results language and
  retained the uncertainty rather than overclaiming its random-citation result.
- Checked for counterevidence to an unqualified “answer first / separate
  detail” prescription: user reports of insufficient detail, non-neutral
  trust effects from citations/uncertainty, and lack of direct comparison of
  complete output formats delimit that prescription.
- Further searching is unlikely to resolve the central gap without a study
  designed to compare report layouts for diverse research consumers. The
  requested evidence and judgment are sufficient for a bounded workflow
  recommendation; no claim of a universal optimum is made.
