# Audit

## Question

What should an LLM establish about a user's goal and context before web
research begins, and when is clarification worth asking? Focus on guidance
that can improve task orientation without encouraging the agent to invent
unstated requirements or ask unnecessary questions.

## Claims and evidence

- **C1 — Supported, with scope limits.** A surface topic or short search query
  can underdetermine the user's information need. A research brief should
  establish the question or task the user wants the evidence to serve. Direct
  evidence comes mainly from information-retrieval research; transfer to an
  LLM research workflow is a reasoned application, not a tested universal
  rule.
- **C2 — Partially supported.** Task context can be used to formulate or refine
  queries, filter/rank results, and choose a search domain. This supports
  eliciting context only when it changes evidence selection or interpretation.
  The specific checklist “intended consumer, constraints, and use” is a design
  recommendation; the sources do not validate each item as universally
  necessary.
- **C3 — Supported as a workflow recommendation, not as a universal threshold.**
  LLM clarification research treats asking as a decision with benefits and
  interaction costs; whether a likely interpretation is dominant and whether
  the user values speed or carefulness affect the decision. Thus ask when
  resolving an uncertainty could materially change the evidence plan or answer,
  and otherwise state a reasonable assumption and proceed. No located study
  directly tests this rule in open-web research tasks.

Do not infer universal LLM behavior from the user's experience. Keep evidence
claims separate from the practical design recommendation below.

### Evidence-backed synthesis

Before web research, the agent should be able to state (a) the user's actual
question, decision, or learning task rather than only the subject area, and
(b) any supplied context that changes what sources count as relevant. This
does not imply interviewing the user for a complete specification. Research
retrieval literature supports that task context can alter query generation,
disambiguation, ranking, filtering, or search-engine choice (E2). Web-search
work documents short queries with multiple possible intents and reports that
clarification can help in that setting (E1). LLM studies separately show that
clarification utility depends on the distribution of possible intents and
interaction preferences (E3), while controlled underspecification evaluations
show that models can miss needed information (E4).

**Design recommendation (inference from the evidence):** ask one targeted
question only when the unresolved answer could change the research question,
scope, source standard, or likely conclusion enough to justify the delay. If
not, proceed with a plainly stated, low-risk assumption. Do not turn possible
preferences or constraints into asserted user requirements. This balances
getting enough context against interaction burden; it is not a directly tested
web-research policy.

### Evidence entries

- **E1: C1, C3 -> S1 -> direct web-search evidence.** Zamani et al. describe
  queries as often short and ambiguous, with only one possible intent applying
  to the current user; they contrast result diversification with asking to
  clarify the information need. Their Bing experiment compared a clarification
  question plus candidate answers to a static prompt plus the same answers,
  reporting 48.57% relative CTR engagement uplift (p < 10^-20) across two
  EN-US treatments of 2.5 million users each. The authors' own paper reports
  user studies and log-derived clarification categories. It establishes that
  clarifying query intent can affect engagement in this search interface; CTR
  does not establish research-answer quality, accuracy, or user benefit in all
  tasks. **Support: direct for ambiguity/intent in web search; indirect for
  LLM research briefing. Confidence: moderate.**
- **E2: C2 -> S2 -> review of task-context retrieval methods.** Maguitman says
  context extracted from a user's task can target task-relevant retrieval and
  can inform automatic queries, query refinement/disambiguation, filtering,
  ranking, and search-engine selection. This supports the relevance of
  task-linked context; it does not show that every contextual field is needed
  for every search or that asking users for it improves an LLM's answer.
  **Support: direct for contextual retrieval functions; indirect for the
  proposed fields and conversational workflow. Confidence: moderate.**
- **E3: C3 -> S3 -> controlled LLM clarification evaluations.** Zhang and Choi
  frame clarification choice around both the user's speed/usability versus
  carefulness preferences and whether one interpretation dominates. Their
  method estimates intent uncertainty and, in three NLP task settings, better
  identifies cases that benefit from clarification than tested uncertainty
  baselines. The paper provides evidence against asking solely because a
  request admits multiple theoretical readings. It does not establish a
  general “materially changes research direction/conclusion” cutoff or test
  web-research tasks. **Support: direct for selective clarification factors;
  indirect for this audit's operational threshold. Confidence: moderate.**
- **E4: C1, C3 -> S4 -> prompt underspecification evaluation.** Yang et al.
  report that tested LLMs sometimes infer omitted requirements, but behavior
  varied across prompts and model versions; their paper also reports that
  specifying every requirement can reduce performance in their tested setup.
  This cautions against assuming the model will infer all user requirements
  and against collecting an exhaustive checklist. Their experiments use
  curated prompts, three generation tasks, selected models, and
  LLM-assisted evaluation; they did not study human-led web-research
  conversations or test whether asking is better than stating assumptions.
  **Support: direct for variability under prompt underspecification; indirect
  for research intake policy. Confidence: moderate-low for transfer.**
- **E5: C3 -> S5 -> recent controlled multi-turn information-seeking
  evaluation.** Huang et al. evaluate 5,251 problems / 9,006 instances across
  mathematics, logic, biology, medicine, and general knowledge. They report
  that tested LLMs often underestimate how much information is missing, fail
  to identify a minimal sufficient query set, and can stop before gathering
  enough information. Their paper explicitly studies underspecification, not
  ambiguity. It establishes that sufficiency checking can be difficult in
  controlled information-acquisition tasks, not that the same rates or
  failure modes hold for open-web research. This is a 2026 arXiv preprint,
  not yet independently replicated in the sources checked. **Support: direct
  for the benchmarked tasks; weakly transferable. Confidence: low-to-moderate
  for this workflow.**

## Conflicts and limitations

- **Transfer boundary:** Search clarification experiments address query intent,
  ranking, and engagement; LLM clarification experiments address selected
  NLP tasks; prompt underspecification work addresses curated generation
  requirements. None directly compares complete LLM web-research workflows
  with and without a structured pre-search brief.
- **Potential tension:** S1's large CTR increase and qualitative user studies
  favor clarification in some search contexts, but S3 frames the choice as
  dependent on likely intent and user preferences, and explicitly includes
  speed/usability costs. These are compatible: benefit is contextual, not a
  mandate to clarify every broad or multi-interpretation query.
- **Do not overgeneralize:** C2's “consumer, constraints, and use” fields are
  practical prompts for finding task context; available evidence does not
  independently validate those named fields as necessary in every research
  task. The operational “could change the research direction or conclusion”
  threshold is synthesis/design judgment, not an empirically calibrated
  decision boundary.
- **Recent source caution:** S5 is an arXiv preprint and its controlled
  constraint-acquisition setting differs from open-web research. S4's results
  are model-, prompt-, and task-dependent and do not report an end-user
  clarification study.
- **Unmet evidence:** No source located directly tests whether explicitly
  establishing intended consumer, constraints, use, geography, or time period
  improves the relevance or accuracy of an LLM's web research, nor quantifies
  when the interaction cost is outweighed by improved research outcomes.

## Validation

Every material claim links to E1-E5 and sources S1-S5. Source publication dates,
provenance, scope, and access dates are recorded. Search claims were checked
against paper full text or official publisher/journal records where available;
the Microsoft Research full-paper copy was checked for the experiment details.
The distinction between evidence and design recommendation is explicit. A
refutation pass considered whether broad clarification is always beneficial:
S3's speed/usability and intent-distribution factors, plus S4's cost of
specifying many requirements, argue against exhaustive intake. No source
directly establishes the precise “materially change direction or conclusion”
rule for web research. Return that gap and the transfer limits to the
coordinator.
