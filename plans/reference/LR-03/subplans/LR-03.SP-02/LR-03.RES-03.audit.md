# Audit

## Question

How should an LLM bound a web-research task into prioritized questions,
evidence needs, and stopping conditions so a broad topic does not dilute
answer quality?

## Claims and evidence

- C1 — Breaking a broad user goal into prioritized research questions can
  improve focus. **Supported with limits.** A controlled QA evaluation found
  gains from decomposition for compositional questions; systematic review
  guidance supports focusing and scoping questions for feasibility and user
  relevance. Neither directly tests LLM-led open-web research workflows or a
  general prioritization protocol.
- C2 — Scope constraints such as time period, geography, and stakes should be
  selected when they can change the answer. **Supported as a conditional
  workflow heuristic, not as a universally tested rule.** Methodological
  guidance ties scope and eligibility choices to review purpose, context,
  applicability, and feasibility. These sources do not establish a single
  checklist of constraints for general web research.
- C3 — Search stopping criteria can be tied to coverage of material questions,
  evidence quality, and remaining uncertainty without claiming complete open
  web recall. **Supported as a bounded workflow heuristic.** Search-methods
  guidance says stopping is often not objectively decidable, suggests
  checking known relevant records and citation paths, and notes weak formal
  evaluation of proposed rules. A retrieval experiment models desired recall
  within a defined collection; it does not demonstrate complete recall on the
  open web.

### Evidence entries

- E1: C1 -> S1 -> The paper reports question decomposition raised
  precision@1 from 20.8 to 27.5 on COMPLEXWEBQUESTIONS (pp. 641, 642; ACL
  Anthology lines 19–20, 91–94). Its system decomposes complex questions into
  simple ones, searches for answers, then composes those answers. This is
  direct empirical evidence that decomposition can improve performance in
  this benchmark. It does not measure user-directed research, prioritization,
  source quality, broad topical research, nor the cost of decomposition; the
  benchmark has generated compositional QA questions and its metric is
  precision@1. Support: bounded / moderate confidence for decomposition in
  complex QA, low transfer confidence to general LLM web research.
- E2: C1, C2 -> S2 -> Cochrane advises defining a review question in relation
  to intended users and purpose, and describes feasibility as avoiding
  questions that retrieve unmanageable quantities; advance scoping helps set
  sensible boundaries (Chapter 2, lines 61–68). It requires advance
  specification of PICO objectives for intervention reviews (lines 88–100).
  Its breadth discussion identifies tradeoffs: broader scope may improve
  comprehensiveness or generalizability but needs more resources and can make
  interpretation difficult; narrow scope improves manageability but can leave
  sparse evidence or miss subgroup differences (lines 106–119). This supports
  purposeful, context-sensitive scope and prioritization, not a universal
  rule to narrow every task. Domain: systematic health intervention reviews.
  Support: direct methodological guidance within scope; moderate transfer
  confidence.
- E3: C2 -> S3 -> PRISMA 2020 asks review authors to state eligibility
  characteristics including study design and setting, dissemination year,
  language, and report status, and to give rationales for notable
  restrictions (Explanation of item 5, lines 523–533). It explains these
  details let readers understand scope and verify inclusion decisions. This
  supports making consequential constraints explicit and justified. It does
  not show that time, geography, or stakes always matter; it is reporting
  guidance for systematic reviews. Support: direct for review transparency,
  indirect for general web-research workflow.
- E4: C3 -> S4 -> Cochrane says search completion is often difficult to decide
  objectively; candidate stopping rules include no new relevant records after
  added terms, but these have had little formal evaluation, while scarce
  evidence calls for more caution (Chapter 4, lines 495–499). Checking key
  known records alone is insufficient; citation/reference checks can help
  assess performance, and additional discoveries may signal a weak initial
  strategy (lines 500–509). Qualitative synthesis may stop when no new
  information emerges, with reasons documented; complex topics may call for
  a different focus than exhaustive identification (lines 510–511). This
  supports explicit, task-sensitive reasons for stopping and states why no
  universal threshold is justified. Domain: evidence-synthesis search, not
  open-web LLM research. Support: direct method guidance, moderate transfer
  confidence.
- E5: C3 -> S5 -> Sneyd and Stevenson propose and evaluate a Poisson-process
  stopping method on a public retrieval dataset; the method allows a user to
  specify a minimum desired recall and probability of reaching it (ACL
  Anthology abstract and bibliographic record, 2019). This shows stopping can
  be formalized against a target when a collection and recall model are
  defined. It does not establish the true recall of a search over the open
  web, whose relevant document population is not enumerated in this setup.
  The audit page's accessible abstract did not expose the paper's detailed
  dataset results, so no performance figures are asserted. Support: bounded
  / moderate for collection-based retrieval stopping; low transfer to open
  web.
- E6: C3 -> S3 -> PRISMA says reporting sources and last-searched dates lets
  readers assess review completeness and currency; reporting full search
  strategies supports transparency, replication, and assessment of likely
  identification (Explanation of items 6–7, lines 541–592). This is evidence
  for auditable scope and search provenance, not a stopping rule. Support:
  direct reporting guidance, indirect for the proposed stopping heuristic.

## Bounded workflow implications (recommendations, not empirical findings)

The evidence supports a practical rule for this skill: first identify the
user's decision or learning need, then extract the few questions whose answers
could materially change that outcome. Rank them by decision relevance and
dependency (what must be known before another question can be resolved); keep
secondary context only if it changes interpretation or applicability. This
ranking procedure is a proposed workflow heuristic informed by E1–E2, not a
directly tested formula.

Select constraints conditionally. Include a time period when currency,
historical change, or a defined evidence window affects the answer; include
geography or population when jurisdiction, context, or applicability may
change findings; reflect stakes in the evidence rigor and validation required.
Record the reason for a material constraint and the likely effect of excluding
it. These examples are proposed operationalizations informed by E2–E3 and the
SP-01 orientation; this research did not test each constraint individually.

Stop when the material questions have evidence adequate for their importance,
meaningful alternatives/refutations have been checked, and another search path
is unlikely to change the conclusion; record unresolved uncertainty and why
searching stopped. Use newly found terminology and useful citation/reference
paths to test coverage when available. Treat “no new useful result in the
latest pass” only as one signal, not proof of completeness. Increase search
effort for scarce evidence or high consequences. This is a task-sensitive
heuristic inferred from E4–E6, not a validated universal stopping threshold.
Open-web recall remains unmeasured.

## Conflicts and limitations

Search recall on the open web is not directly measurable in this assignment.
Stop criteria are task-dependent; the evidence does not justify a universal
threshold or a claim of exhaustive coverage. Empirical question-decomposition
results come from a generated compositional QA benchmark. Cochrane and PRISMA
guidance concerns systematic reviews, where exhaustive study identification
and reproducibility requirements differ from ordinary user-directed web
research. The recommendations below are workflow heuristics informed by
these sources, not empirically validated policies for general LLM research.
