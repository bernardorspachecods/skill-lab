# Audit

## Question

What workflow makes an LLM extract relevant evidence from inspected sources
and turn it into direct, supported findings rather than a collection of source
summaries?

## Conclusion

Use a question- and claim-led evidence record. For each prioritized question,
state the specific claim or information need, then inspect the source and save
the passage, table, data point, or other exact location that bears on it. Link
each evidence item to the claim it supports, contradicts, or leaves unresolved;
record what that material establishes and what it cannot establish from its
scope, method, or wording. When no supporting passage is found, retain that as
an evidence gap rather than filling it with a source summary. After extraction,
synthesize across evidence items to answer the question, showing where the
answer is directly reported and where it combines sources or relies on
inference. Keep recommendations as a further judgment that states its basis
and assumptions.

This workflow is a **design recommendation**, not a fully validated recipe for
general-purpose web research. Direct LLM evaluations show that decomposing
claim and evidence extraction into stages can improve claim-evidence retrieval
recall on a narrow scientific-paper benchmark, while also increasing cost and
sometimes sacrificing precision. Separate attribution evaluations show that
LLM answers can cite irrelevant or inadequate sources, even when the source
domain looks plausible. Method guidance for evidence synthesis recommends
question-aligned extraction, valid conclusions, and explicit evidence
certainty, but addresses systematic reviews and guideline development.

Confidence: **moderate** that question-linked, source-located evidence records
are a sound workflow recommendation; **low** that recording an explicit
“establishes / does not establish” field by itself prevents overclaiming or
improves reader comprehension. The reviewed studies do not directly compare
that annotation practice against otherwise identical workflows. Evidence is
also limited for arbitrary open-web research, non-English sources, or
cross-domain tasks outside the evaluated settings.

## Evidence entries

**E1: C1, C2 -> S1 -> exact support.** Javaji et al., “Can AI Validate
Science? Benchmarking LLMs on Claim → Evidence Reasoning in AI Papers,”
*Proceedings of IJCNLP-AACL 2025*, pp. 2355–2379, published December 2025;
accessed 30 September 2026. [ACL Anthology paper and PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2025.ijcnlp-long.127/).
The paper defines CLAIM-BENCH as jointly testing claim extraction, evidence
extraction, and claim-evidence-link validation (PDF pp. 1, 4). Its comparison
describes a staged three-pass method—claims, corresponding evidence, then
conclusions—and a one-by-one method that retrieves evidence for each claim
(PDF p. 4, lines 347–369). On its 100 recent AI/ML papers and 346 manually
annotated claim-evidence pairs, it reports that iterative prompts retrieve
over four times as many pairs as single-pass prompting, substantially
increasing recall; the abstract reports a best claim-identification F1 of
0.59 (PDF p. 1, lines 18–30). The prompt templates request exact quotations,
source locations, evidence type/strength, and limitations, and specify an
empty evidence list when none is found (Appendix pp. 11–15, especially lines
928–965 and 994–1019). **Establishes:** explicit claim-to-evidence extraction
is a measurable task; on this benchmark, decomposed prompts found more gold
pairs than single-pass prompts, and authors designed their extraction schema
to preserve locations and limitations. **Does not establish:** that this
specific schema improves factuality, that listing limits prevents
overclaiming, or that the benchmark result transfers to open-web research,
other disciplines, or a reader-facing answer. Results show a precision-recall
trade-off, with high-recall settings sometimes adding false positives (PDF
p. 5, lines 373–394); one-by-one prompting costs more time and compute (PDF
p. 4, lines 361–367). **Quality/role:** primary peer-reviewed benchmark and
model evaluation. **Independence:** standalone benchmark and author team;
results are not an independent replication by another lab. Scope is recent,
non-math-intensive papers under 20 pages, annotated by four experienced PhD
students (PDF p. 3, lines 181–210), not diverse web-source types.

**E2: C1, C2, C3 -> S2 -> exact support.** Wei et al., “EXPERTQA: Expert-Curated
Questions and Attributed Answers,” *Proceedings of NAACL 2024*, pp. 3025–3045;
accessed 30 September 2026. [ACL Anthology paper and PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.167/).
Experts created information-seeking questions across 32 fields and judged
answer claims separately for informativeness to the question, cite-worthiness,
factuality, faithfulness to accompanying evidence, and reliability of the
evidence source; experts then revised claims to be faithful to reliable
evidence (PDF p. 2, lines 93–115; Table 3, p. 3). Across sampled systems,
retrieve-and-read systems produced more complete attributions than prompted
and post-hoc approaches, but missed citations for 18% of cite-worthy claims;
post-hoc systems supplied citations by design but more often had incomplete
attributions (PDF p. 4, lines 335–346). The paper also reports that over 60%
of GPT-4's citations generated without retrieval pointed to plausible
domains but mismatched webpage content (PDF p. 4, lines 343–347). **Establishes:**
relevance to the question, factuality, evidence faithfulness, citation
completeness, and source reliability are separable evaluation dimensions;
retrieving or attaching a source alone does not ensure support. **Does not
establish:** that writing these dimensions as output labels causes better
answers or clearer reader understanding, or that the sampled systems represent
all current models. **Quality/role:** primary expert-annotated evaluation of
LLM-attributed answers, including retrieval-augmented and post-hoc systems.
**Independence:** different authors, task, and benchmark from S1/S3. Limitations
include sentence-level claims that are often non-atomic, gaps in field
coverage, and a participant/question sample centered on English speakers in
Europe, the Americas, and Africa (PDF p. 9, lines 628–649). This evaluation
supports the need to check exact content and question relevance, not a
universal quality estimate.

**E3: C1 -> S3 -> exact support.** Buchmann, Liu, and Gurevych, “Attribute or
Abstain: Large Language Models as Long Document Assistants,” *Proceedings of
EMNLP 2024*, pp. 8113–8140, November 2024; accessed 30 September 2026.
[ACL Anthology paper](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.463/). The paper
evaluates six long-document tasks and five LLMs. It reports that producing a
response and extracting its evidence in one step performed best for large and
fine-tuned models, while additional retrieval helped smaller prompted models;
evidence quality predicted answer quality on simple-response datasets, but
not on complex-response datasets, where models struggled to provide evidence
for complex claims (abstract, publisher record lines 57–60). **Establishes:**
evidence extraction can be integrated with response generation, but which
workflow performs better depends on model setup and task complexity; evidence
quality alone was not a reliable proxy for complex-answer quality in this
evaluation. **Does not establish:** a general optimum between one-step and
separate extraction, a web research workflow, or that evidence cards improve
answer quality. **Quality/role:** primary peer-reviewed long-document
attribution benchmark. **Independence:** distinct authors and data from S1
and S2. The experiment's six tasks and controlled document setting limit
transfer to web research.

**E4: C1, C2, C3 -> S4 -> exact support.** Australian National Health and
Medical Research Council (NHMRC), “Synthesising evidence,” *Guidelines for
Guidelines* online methods manual; accessed 30 September 2026. The page has no
visible publication or update date in the opened record. [NHMRC guidance](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/guidelinesforguidelines/develop/synthesising-evidence).
The manual describes synthesis as beginning with systematic collection of
descriptive and outcome data. It says good synthesis should use systematic
methods, make decisions clear, assess evidence certainty, and report only the
overall conclusions that can validly be drawn; it explicitly says synthesis
“does not simply collate and list the findings” (lines 14–23, 49). The page
also says questions should set priorities for synthesis, comparisons, evidence
types, and outcomes (lines 27–38). **Establishes:** authoritative practice
guidance for question-aligned extraction, synthesis rather than source-by-source
listing, and communicating certainty. **Does not establish:** this process's
effect on LLM performance or its fit as a complete workflow for ordinary web
research. **Quality/role:** national health research agency methods guidance
for guideline developers and evidence reviews; not an experimental study.
**Independence:** institutionally and methodologically distinct from LLM
benchmark research; scope is formal evidence synthesis.

**E5: C2, C3 -> S5 -> exact support.** Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI), “4.4.10
Conclusions and recommendations,” *JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis*, updated
5 May 2025; accessed 30 September 2026. [JBI manual section](https://jbi-global.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/MANUAL/pages/355828997/4.4.10%2BConclusions%2Band%2Brecommendations).
The guidance says conclusions should directly answer review questions and be
based on results/direct inference from them; recommendations for practice are
inferred from the results plus generalizability and applicability factors,
while research recommendations may derive from identified limitations (lines
122–143). **Establishes:** a concrete methodological distinction between
review results/conclusions and downstream recommendations, including
applicability judgments. **Does not establish:** that typographic labels
improve clarity or calibration for readers, or that systematic-review
conventions should be applied wholesale to LLM web research. **Quality/role:**
current professional evidence-synthesis manual. **Independence:** complements
NHMRC guidance but shares the broader systematic-review methods tradition;
neither is an LLM intervention study.

## Synthesis, uncertainty, and refutation

1. **Target extraction to the question or claim.** This is directly supported
   as a useful experimental task design: CLAIM-BENCH evaluates claim extraction,
   supporting-passage extraction, and their links, and reports that iterative
   decomposition increased retrieved pairs and recall versus single pass on
   its benchmark. NHMRC guidance independently starts synthesis design from
   the questions, comparisons, evidence types, and outcomes. Together they
   justify a question-led extraction record as a practical design
   recommendation. They do not show that every research task needs atomic
   claim decomposition, or that more retrieved passages always improve final
   answers.
2. **Record source-local limits alongside support.** CLAIM-BENCH's extraction
   schema asks for exact quotations and locations, labels evidence type and
   strength, captures limitations, and allows “no evidence” as an explicit
   output. This is a design instantiated by the paper, not an isolated
   evaluation of each field's value. ExpertQA demonstrates why it matters to
   inspect the exact passage: attribution errors persisted, including
   plausible-domain links whose page contents did not match the generated
   claim. It did not experimentally establish that recording limitations
   reduces overclaiming. Therefore “what this establishes / does not establish”
   is a traceability and calibration recommendation, with no quantified
   effect estimate.
3. **Synthesize across evidence rather than restate one source at a time.**
   The NHMRC guidance explicitly rejects a synthesis that merely lists study
   findings and asks for valid overall conclusions and certainty. The
   practical next step is to group linked evidence under prioritized questions,
   note convergence/conflict, and answer only to the scope those records
   jointly support. This is an inference from evidence-synthesis practice;
   this investigation found no controlled LLM experiment directly comparing
   source-by-source summaries with that synthesis workflow.
4. **Keep factual attribution and recommendation reasoning distinct.**
   EXPERTQA's annotation framework treats informativeness, factuality,
   evidence-faithfulness, source reliability, and cite-worthiness as distinct
   judgments. JBI guidance says review conclusions answer the review question
   from results, while recommendations add generalizability and applicability
   reasoning. Use these as separate answer layers: (a) source-reported
   evidence, (b) cross-source synthesis or inference, and (c) recommendation
   with assumptions/values or applicability factors. This separation is a
   well-grounded writing/method convention; no reviewed trial measured reader
   clarity or calibration from explicit labels.
5. **Preserve uncertainty and inspect both missing support and false
   positives.** CLAIM-BENCH reports a precision-recall trade-off: prompting
   that retrieves more distant pairs can also introduce false positives.
   EXPERTQA finds that retrieval-augmented systems may omit citations for
   cite-worthy claims and post-hoc attribution may attach incomplete ones.
   LAB further reports that evidence quality predicts response quality for
   simple but not complex responses. Thus absence of an extracted passage,
   incomplete support, and task complexity should remain visible; confidence
   should not be inferred from citation count or a high retrieval yield.

**Conflicts and limits.** The most material counterpoint to a rigid staged
workflow is LAB's result that joint answer generation and evidence extraction
performed best for larger/fine-tuned models, while extra retrieval helped
smaller prompted models. CLAIM-BENCH, by contrast, found gains from iterative
decomposition on its AI-paper benchmark. These are not direct contradictions:
they evaluate different tasks, inputs, model configurations, and outcomes.
They argue for a question/complexity-sensitive workflow, not a universal
single-pass or multi-pass rule. Similarly, the greater recall in CLAIM-BENCH
comes with computational expense and, in some settings, lower precision.

**Stop reason.** The three claims are answered to the extent the evidence
allows: primary LLM evaluations support claim-level extraction and demonstrate
attribution failure modes; methods manuals support careful synthesis,
certainty, and separation of conclusions from recommendations. A remaining
gap is the absence of a direct ablation showing that recording “what the
evidence does not establish” prevents overclaiming or improves reader
calibration in general web research. Further broad searching is unlikely to
resolve that empirical gap without a directly relevant comparative study.

- C1 links to E1–E4; it is marked partial because benchmark gains are specific
  to evaluated claim-evidence tasks, and the general workflow is a design
  recommendation.
- C2 links to E1, E2, E4, and E5; it explicitly states that the protective
  effect of a limitations field is untested.
- C3 links to E2, E4, and E5; evidence supports the separate dimensions and
  methods convention, but no evidence found measures resulting reader clarity.
- The retained empirical claims were checked in original ACL Anthology
  publisher records/PDFs, including methods, results, and stated limitations;
  guidance claims were checked in the NHMRC and JBI pages. No search snippet
  was used as evidence.
- Provenance clusters are separate: CLAIM-BENCH, EXPERTQA, and LAB have distinct
  research teams and benchmarks; NHMRC and JBI are separate guidance producers
  but share the broader evidence-synthesis tradition. No duplicate URLs were
  counted as independent studies.
- The refutation pass checked task complexity, missing/incomplete attributions,
  precision-recall/cost trade-offs, and the result that evidence quality may
  not predict complex-answer quality. These tests bound the recommendation;
  they do not demonstrate a universal effect.
- No source was found that directly compares explicit limitation logging
  versus no limitation logging for prevention of overclaiming. This remains an
  unmet empirical requirement, not a reason to state the recommendation as a
  measured result.
- No source-page instruction or promotional framing changed the assigned
  question.
