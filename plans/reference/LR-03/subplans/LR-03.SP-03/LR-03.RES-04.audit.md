# Audit

## Question

How can an LLM plan useful web search paths and identify valuable sites or
source types for a bounded research question, while distinguishing discovery
leads, source quality, and support for exact claims?

## Conclusion

The evidence supports a question-led, revisable source map, not a generic
whitelist of prestigious domains. Start by translating the bounded question
into the records and source roles likely to answer it (for example: the
governing regulation for current legal requirements, an original dataset or
study for an empirical result, an official registry for ongoing or unpublished
records, or original reporting for a reported event). Select and retain a
resource only when its scope, coverage, and access fit the question; revise the
map when searching reveals a missing record type or an unproductive route.
This is a synthesis of methods guidance, not a tested universal algorithm for
LLMs.

Use a baseline search, then a small set of traceable expansions grounded in
the question or terms observed in relevant records. For hard-to-search topics,
backward and forward citation paths can reveal records missed by keyword
searches; use them as supplements, since their yield varies and they do not
establish completeness. Search for alternatives and disconfirmation by looking
for corrections, limitations, null or contrary results, and competing
explanations. Keep discovery candidates separate from evidence: inspect the
actual record and verify that it supports the precise claim. A producer's
accountability, method, provenance, currency, and incentives inform source
quality, but they do not by themselves establish claim support.

Confidence: **moderate** for the general workflow recommendation, grounded in
search-method guidance and retrieval evaluations; **limited** for its
performance as a general-purpose LLM web-research algorithm. The available LLM
benchmarks focus on academic paper search or fixed retrieval datasets, not
open-web research across arbitrary topics.

## Evidence entries

**E1: C1, C3 -> S1 -> exact support.** Stansfield, Dickson, and Bangpan,
“Exploring issues
in the conduct of website searching and other online sources for systematic
reviews: how can we be systematic?” *Systematic Reviews* 5, article 191
(2016), published 15 November 2016; accessed 30 September 2026.
[Publisher article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13643-016-0371-9).
The authors say selection of appropriate websites “is more dependent on the
precise nature of the research question” and knowledge/accessibility of
websites (article lines 74–82); resource choices can change if new sources
emerge or selected resources prove unhelpful. They also distinguish planning,
execution, and relevance screening, and recommend transparent rationale and
records (lines 68–72, 89–94, 135–136). **Establishes:** topic- and purpose-led
resource selection, review, and documenting the choice in systematic-review
website searches. **Does not establish:** that LLMs can do this reliably, a
cross-domain source-selection algorithm, or a universal source hierarchy.
**Quality/role:** methods discussion by experienced systematic-review
searchers in multiple fields; useful authoritative-method guidance, but
experience-informed recommendations rather than a controlled LLM evaluation.
**Independence:** independent of the LLM-agent benchmark evidence (S3/S4).

**E2: C1 -> S2 -> exact support.** PRISMA-S, Rethlefsen et al., “PRISMA-S:
an extension to the PRISMA Statement for Reporting Literature Searches in
Systematic Reviews,” *Systematic Reviews* 10, 39 (2021), first published
26 January 2021; accessed 30 September 2026. [Publisher article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13643-020-01542-z).
Its explanation says no single database provides a complete and accurate list
because records and indexing differ, and suggests broad, specialist, and
regional databases (lines 152–158). It calls for identifying study registries
when those match the evidence need; registries may expose ongoing or unpublished
studies (lines 179–189). It also calls for documenting which websites were
searched and how (lines 194–212), and which seed references and citation
methods were used (lines 225–237). **Establishes:** source types and search
surfaces should be selected for the records/question and transparently
identified; complementary resource roles can fill coverage gaps. **Does not
establish:** the same search requirements for ordinary web research or that
source count alone guarantees completeness. **Quality/role:** consensus
reporting guideline with elaboration for systematic reviews. **Independence:**
methodologically complementary to S1; both concern evidence-synthesis search
practice, so they are not independent experimental replications.

**E3: C1, C3 -> S3 -> exact support.** He et al., “PaSa: An LLM Agent for
Comprehensive Academic Paper Search,” in *Proceedings of ACL 2025*, pp.
11663–11679; published July 2025; accessed 30 September 2026. [ACL
Anthology record and abstract](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.572/),
DOI 10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.572. The abstract describes an agent that
invokes search tools, reads papers, and selects relevant references; on its
RealScholarQuery benchmark, PaSa-7B reports gains over specified Google-based
baselines of 37.78% recall@20 and 39.90% recall@50 (ACL record, abstract and
bibliographic metadata). **Establishes:** a bounded academic-paper-search
agent can combine search and reference-following decisions and was evaluated
on a benchmark of real-world scholar queries. **Does not establish:** success
for arbitrary web topics, the appropriateness or quality of every retrieved
source, or that recall scores show exact claims are supported. The reported
benchmark and comparison are from the system paper; no independent replication
was located in this search. **Quality/role:** primary system/evaluation paper,
published in ACL proceedings. **Independence:** distinct from the review-method
guidelines; performance claim is authored by the evaluated system's
researchers, so it should be treated as a bounded primary evaluation.

**E4: C2 -> S4 -> exact support.** Jagerman et al., “Query Expansion by
Prompting Large Language Models,” 2023 conference paper, version posted to
arXiv 5 May 2023; accessed 30 September 2026. [arXiv paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03653).
The paper says expansions can recover relevant documents lacking lexical
overlap; reports experiments on MS MARCO and BEIR, finding some LLM prompts
outperform traditional query-expansion methods. It also states the limits:
the study evaluated BM25 sparse retrieval, does not directly establish
benefits to dense retrieval, examines specific prompt templates, and notes
computational cost (paper §§1, 4–5; lines 11–18, 73–74, 190–200 in extracted
HTML). **Establishes:** query expansion can help retrieval under controlled
benchmark conditions, and prompt choice matters. **Does not establish:** that
free-form expansion improves live web research or that gains transfer to other
search systems and tasks. **Quality/role:** primary retrieval experiment;
arXiv is the paper's record, not independent confirmation. **Independence:**
distinct author team and experimental setup from S5, but both are related
LLM-query-expansion research and should not be counted as broad real-world
replications.

**E5: C2 -> S5 -> exact support.** Wang, Yang, and Wei, “Query2doc: Query

## Synthesis and uncertainty

1. **Map source roles from what would answer the question.** The question
   determines relevant record types, jurisdiction/population/time coverage,
   and whether a source is primary, governing, or explanatory. The site-search
   methods paper specifically cautions that resource selection depends on the
   question and accessible resources and may need revision during searching.
   Treat this as sound workflow guidance with systematic-review origins, not
   as proof that one taxonomy covers every research task.
2. **Expand queries in controlled, observable steps.** Preserve the baseline;
   add likely synonyms, official terms, entities, and alternatives, noting
   where each came from. Empirical LLM query-expansion studies show retrieval
   gains on selected IR test sets but cannot establish gains for arbitrary web
   research. Query expansion can also add terms that increase irrelevant
   matches; inspect results and keep/reject expansions based on relevance to
   the original question. The last sentence is a general retrieval-method
   inference, not a quantified result from these papers.
3. **Use references and citations as paths to records.** Backward paths often
   expose foundational/original records; forward paths can surface newer
   records, but usefulness depends on topic and indexing. TARCiS reports
   variable unique-record yield and recommends citation searching as a
   supplement for difficult systematic-review topics, with transparent seed,
   direction, date, iteration, and stop reporting. Do not turn citation links
   into corroboration counts: linked pages may rely on the same underlying
   source, and citation relationships alone do not establish agreement or
   quality.
4. **Search for evidence that could change the answer.** Deliberately seek
   source-native limitations, corrections, null results, contrary results,
   and competing explanations. The reviewed sources support documenting
   alternatives and corrections as method practice; they do not supply a
   controlled experiment measuring how much this step improves general LLM
   research accuracy.
5. **Evaluate a source, then separately test its support.** Assess producer,
   provenance, method, scope, incentives, currency/version, and independent
   corroboration in relation to the question. Then inspect the underlying
   passage, data, or record for the exact claim. AIS directly supports treating
   output-to-source attribution as its own evaluation relation, but broader
   source-quality dimensions are a synthesis of research-method practice and
   should not be presented as a validated scoring formula. A search rank or
   official status can make a page a valuable lead or authoritative record for
   a narrow kind of fact; it does not ensure the passage proves the sentence.

### Refutation, conflicts, and stop

- The strongest apparent counterexample to question-led expansion is that
  LLM query expansion has positive benchmark results and a specific paper
  reports benefits on both sparse and dense retrieval. This does not justify
  unrestricted fan-out: experiments use known benchmark tasks and retrieval
  measures, not a full research workflow or exact truth/claim support.
- Citation chasing can add unique records but its yield varies; the guidance
  specifically rejects standalone use where complete systematic-review recall
  is required. Treat it as a supplementary route, not an exhaustive search.
- Google describes signals including PageRank and other multi-system signals,
  but this is a vendor's description of ranking. It does not validate the
  premise that ranking or domain reputation is an evidence-quality measure.
- No conflicting result in the inspected evidence directly refutes the
  bounded recommendations. S4/S5 support expansion in particular retrieval
  experiments; they do not contradict the caution that expansion must be
  controlled and checked.
- Stop reason: all three claims have direct support for the applicable method
  context, their main scope limits/refutations are recorded, and another broad
  search is unlikely to establish general-purpose LLM web-research efficacy.
  The material remaining gap is the absence of independent, cross-topic
  evaluations of full LLM workflows measuring both source mapping and exact
  claim support.
