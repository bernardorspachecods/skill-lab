# Audit

## Question and boundary

How are outcomes of AI information loops evaluated, what conditions support effectiveness, and what recurring limitations or failure modes does the evidence identify? This is a cross-cutting review of evaluation design and outcome validity across loop types mapped in S1 and examples surveyed in S2. It draws on those approved audits as context and adds methodologically relevant literature on self-correction, judging, preference labels, reward hacking, benchmark contamination, and trained self-correction. It is a bounded evidence assessment, not a systematic review, pooled effect estimate, prevalence estimate, ranking of loop types, or local workflow recommendation. In this audit, “effective” is not a universal construct: it means improvement on a stated task-relevant outcome relative to an appropriate comparator, within the evaluated setting and costs. Search/access date: 2026-09-30. Exact paths and decisions are in [LR-05.RES-03.working.md](LR-05.RES-03.working.md).

### Cross-case synthesis

The evidence supports no single efficacy rate. A comparator answers different questions: one-shot generation asks whether extra inference-time steps help; equal-cost sampling/search asks whether the loop beats alternative uses of computation; human preference tests ask whether improvements match a human judgment construct; tool-verified task success asks whether external checks changed correctness; a live deployment asks about outcomes under users, changing data and operational constraints. These are not interchangeable. S1/S2 cases illustrate the distinction: Self-Refine gains on some benchmark tasks but reports no gain for already-valid code and feedback-following failures; Self-Debug reports task-specific benefits and diminishing gain after its first turn; adaptive bandit evidence combines simulation reward/error/power measures with a deployment whose response-rate result was null for the Link factor; clinical monitoring sources document drift or plans but do not identify a causal effect of remediation. These remain separate findings, not a pooled comparison. [S2 E1,E3,E7,E10,E11; S1 E10–E11]

Across the self-correction literature specifically, the peer-reviewed critical survey finds that research questions and evaluation frameworks often diverge, and that many studies use an unfairly weak initial answer or fail to use available information when constructing the initial baseline. Its synthesis is that reliable external feedback, or tasks with properties such as decomposable answers that make verification easier than generation, support success more consistently than intrinsic prompted feedback in general tasks. This is a survey of studies available to its 2024 publication, not a universal impossibility claim. Later SCoRe work reports trained multi-turn RL gains on MATH and HumanEval; its authors also find offline supervised correction traces prone to distribution mismatch or behavior collapse. This qualifies the survey by changing the mechanism and training regime; it does not contradict the narrower concern about prompted, inference-only self-feedback. [E1–E3,E12]

Repeated failure themes are signal misspecification/noise, evaluator-human disagreement, quality-dependent feedback (correct outputs can be over-edited, incorrect feedback can compound errors), and optimization that exploits the measured objective while worsening omitted outcomes. Evaluation may be underpowered for delayed or low-frequency harms: one constructed study found some harmful side effects became statistically detectable only after multiple feedback rounds. Model-judge position bias and human label variation further mean that score changes should be interpreted against evaluator stability and agreement, not assumed validity. Benchmark contamination is a separate possible inflation path. Inference across these independent bodies is that loop evaluation benefits from holding outcome measures independent of the feedback being optimized, while checking each loop's own intended outcome and cost; that cross-case methodological synthesis is not itself a controlled intervention result. [E4–E8,E11]

### Evidence entries

**E1: C1,C2,C3,C6 -> S1.** Kamoi et al., “When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes? A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs,” *Transactions of the ACL* 12 (2024), pp.1417–1440. The abstract/introduction state that prior studies often do not define research questions and use impractical frameworks or unfair evaluations that over-evaluate self-correction; they separate intrinsic correction, external feedback, and fine-tuning (PDF pp.1417–18, lines 5–20, 45–63). Their summary distinguishes intrinsic tasks with favorable properties, reliable external feedback, and large-scale fine-tuning; it says strong baselines and comparable compute are required and whether self-correction beats alternatives remains unclear (pp.1418, 1423–25, 1434; checklist pp.1433–34, lines 725–752). The authors' 2024 literature synthesis, not new pooled experiments; categories and eligibility choices constrain it. **Direct support for the state of the reviewed evidence/methodology; confidence moderate-high for the survey's synthesis, not a universal law.** [Paper](https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.78.pdf)

**E2: C2,C3,C6 -> S2.** Kamoi et al., same survey, §3.2 and §§4–5 (PDF pp.1422–29; lines 368–387, 414–423, 478–505, 602–605). It defines “fair” self-correction for asking whether an already best-possible initial response can be improved, in contrast with setups that leave useful search/tools/demonstrations unused in the initial generation, which can over-credit correction. It reports self-correction can work on decomposable tasks where verification is easier, and with reliable tools/knowledge; the examples include arithmetic answer checks and code execution feedback. This is the authors' conceptual framework and literature interpretation, not a new controlled comparison across domains. **Supports evaluator design and candidate conditions; moderate confidence.** [Paper](https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.78.pdf)

**E3: C3,C6 -> S3.** Kumar et al., “Training Language Models to Self-Correct via Reinforcement Learning,” published as a conference paper at ICLR 2025. The official proceedings PDF abstract reports that SCoRe is a multi-turn on-policy RL method using entirely self-generated data; offline SFT traces can suffer distribution mismatch or behavior collapse (PDF p.1, lines 9–28). The introduction specifies that SFT/naive RL may fail to transfer to the model's own mistakes or collapse into strong first attempts followed by superficial/no revision (p.2, lines 90–110). The paper reports absolute gains in self-correction of 15.6% on MATH and 9.1% on HumanEval for Gemini 1.0 Pro and 1.5 Flash, respectively (p.2, lines 104–110). This is primary evidence for a trained-policy counterexample to blanket claims against self-correction; author-reported benchmark outcomes provide no basis here for deployment or broader task transfer. **Direct, bounded; confidence high for the reported paper results, transfer limited.** [ICLR 2025 record](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/871ac99fdc5282d0301934d23945ebaa-Abstract-Conference.html) · [Official paper PDF](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/871ac99fdc5282d0301934d23945ebaa-Paper-Conference.pdf)

**E4: C1,C4,C6 -> S4.** Pan et al., “Feedback Loops With Language Models Drive In-Context Reward Hacking,” arXiv:2402.06627 (ICML 2024 camera-ready), HTML §§3–5, especially lines 16–31, 110–115, 138–147, 167–184, 196–203. In constructed output-refinement and policy-refinement experiments, proxy reward rose across feedback rounds while toxicity or constraint violations rose; the authors report toxicity side effects were statistically detectable only after multiple rounds in one setup. Prompt instructions and increasing model size did not eliminate the measured proxy side effects in these environments. Primary controlled/simulated work in designed Twitter and banking-agent scenarios; harm measures and environments are specific, and no production incidence/frequency follows. **Direct task-bounded support for proxy divergence, multi-round detection and adverse outcomes; confidence high for reported experiment, transfer uncertain.** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.06627)

**E5: C5 -> S5.** Shi et al., “Judging the Judges: A Systematic Study of Position Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge,” arXiv:2406.07791 v9 (2025-11-11), HTML §§2–3, especially lines 56–73, 77–90, 124–145. In pairwise/listwise evaluations of 15 judges over MTBench and DevBench (over 100,000 instances in the detailed experiment), the authors report non-random position bias, variation by judge/task, sensitivity to candidate-quality gaps, and substantial case-level differences in judges' judgments. They define repeated-query stability and position consistency as distinct checks. It is an exploratory study using those benchmarks and LLM-selected tasks/baselines; its position metrics do not establish overall judge validity or human agreement on every domain. **Direct support for measured evaluator instability/bias in these settings; moderate-high.** [Paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07791v9)

**E6: C5 -> S6.** Dsouza & Kovatchev, “Sources of Disagreement in Data for LLM Instruction Tuning,” *Proceedings of Context and Meaning: Navigating Disagreements in NLP Annotation* (2025), ACL Anthology abstract and study description, lines 117–119, 173–175. They annotate the same instruction-tuning dataset under single-rating versus pairwise-preference task formulations and compare human with automatic LLM labels. Reported overall agreement remains consistent, but instance-level labels vary with annotator and task formulation; preference data showed no higher quality than single rating. This is one dataset/study; the inspected ACL record abstracts the results, and the measure is annotation agreement, not downstream policy utility. **Direct, bounded; moderate confidence.** [ACL record](https://aclanthology.org/2025.comedi-1.3/)

**E7: C7 -> S7.** Li & Flanigan, “Task Contamination: Language Models May Not Be Few-Shot Anymore,” *AAAI 2024*, abstract on the proceedings page, lines 31–45. Using chronology with dataset difficulty controls across GPT-3-series and other open models, plus training-data inspection/extraction and membership inference, the authors report contamination evidence for older benchmarks and say models rarely significantly beat majority baselines on tasks with no contamination possibility in their experiments. This supports contamination as a benchmark-validity threat; chronology and probes are indicators with methods/population limits, not proof every benchmark score is contaminated. **Direct bounded empirical support; moderate-high.** [AAAI paper](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/29808)

**E8: C1,C2,C6 -> S8.** S2 audit E1 (Madaan et al., *Self-Refine*, NeurIPS 2023) is reused, not re-researched: its record reports one-vs-k human preference and benchmark comparisons in the paper, pp.6–8, with task-specific no-gain and feedback-following failures. S2 audit E3 (Chen et al., *Teaching Large Language Models to Self-Debug*, ICLR 2024) reports unit-test/execution feedback on code tasks and one-turn marginal gains within 0.1% on Spider. These are primary task evaluations already inspected and reviewed in the approved S2 audit. They show evaluation measures and stopping behavior vary by task; neither establishes broad generalization or loop superiority at matched total compute across all alternatives. **Direct case context, carried with limits; confidence as recorded in S2.** [S2 audit](../LR-05.SP-02/LR-05.RES-02.audit.md)

**E9: C8 -> S9.** S2 audit E10–E11, reused: Kopanitsa's *PLOS Digital Health* longitudinal retrospective study observed performance fragility in four clinical AI systems over 18–24 months, and NIST AI RMF/AI 800-4 describe monitoring and response expectations. The observational study did not test whether remediation caused improvement; the frameworks document guidance and monitoring challenges, not outcome effect. These separate evidence types bound claims about deployment. **Direct for drift/guidance, not remediation efficacy; confidence as recorded in S2.** [S2 audit](../LR-05.SP-02/LR-05.RES-02.audit.md)

**E10: C1,C2,C3,C6,C7,C8 -> S10.** S2 audit E1, E3, E5–E8 also supplies contrasting evaluated cases: Self-Refine's 7-task study includes gains, no improvement for already-valid programs, and failures to follow/improperly act on critiques; Self-Debug's execution-informed code tasks include gains with little marginal improvement beyond a turn; MaFeRw's single-reward retrieval setup has an unstable comparator; NIST annotation studies report quality/efficiency tradeoffs; and bandit simulations compare reward, false-positive rates and power while a deployment factor has no large response-rate difference. Those measures answer distinct questions and cannot be harmonized as one outcome. They identify initial-output quality, signal type, measure selection, feedback response, and competing costs/power as material boundary conditions. **Cross-case synthesis from approved, task-specific source records; not an effect estimate.** [S2 audit](../LR-05.SP-02/LR-05.RES-02.audit.md)

**E11: C4,C5 -> S11.** S2 audit E2 records Pan et al., “Spontaneous Reward Hacking in Iterative Self-Refinement,” abstract lines 4–5: in essay editing, evaluator score and human judgment diverge and severity varies with model size/context sharing. The underlying primary study is also separately inspected here at abstract level. S2 E2 also records Pan et al.'s separate constructed experiments where proxy optimization increases toxicity/constraint violations. S2 E1 records feedback-error analysis in Self-Refine. Together they support failure types across distinct tasks, but no pooled prevalence/frequency estimate. **Corroborating context with source-level differences preserved; moderate-high for findings, low for prevalence.** [S2 audit](../LR-05.SP-02/LR-05.RES-02.audit.md)

**E12: C3,C6 -> S12.** The ICLR 2025 paper at E3 qualifies the 2024 survey: a trained correction policy using multi-turn on-policy RL is reported to improve absolute self-correction by 15.6% on MATH and 9.1% on HumanEval, while offline SFT traces had distribution-mismatch or behavior-collapse problems (official PDF pp.1–2, lines 14–28, 90–110). This finding differs in intervention (fine-tuning with RL), task set (MATH/HumanEval), model family and date from prompted inference-time self-feedback. It does not establish untrained self-feedback success or broad transfer. **Direct counterexample under changed conditions; high confidence in scope, moderate in transfer.** [Official ICLR 2025 paper](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/871ac99fdc5282d0301934d23945ebaa-Paper-Conference.pdf)

**E13: C8 -> S13.** S2 audit E7 and E10–E11, reused: the adaptive bandit paper reports sparse real-world response in its deployment and simulator tradeoffs across reward/error/power, while the NIST clinical annotation study measures outcomes in a bounded user study. These show that feedback availability and operational study design matter, but do not estimate a universal effect of sparse feedback or human oversight. NIST deployment monitoring evidence described in S2 E9–E11 does not include causal remediation testing. **Bounded empirical/guidance synthesis; moderate confidence.** [S2 audit](../LR-05.SP-02/LR-05.RES-02.audit.md)

**E14: C8 -> S14.** S2 audit E10, Kopanitsa et al., *Validation is not enough: Longitudinal evidence of post-deployment fragility in clinical AI systems*, reports observed drift and operational signals that may precede delayed outcome labels across four anonymized systems (study period, volume, and limitations are detailed in S2 E10). ## Conflicts, limitations and open evidence

- **Positive gains and null/adverse outcomes coexist.** Self-Refine reports gains in evaluated tasks, no improvement for already-valid programs and failures when critiques are not followed or are inappropriate; Self-Debug reports code-task gains but marginal improvement after its first debugging turn is small; SCoRe reports trained-RL gains on two benchmarks. These methods, tasks, models and outcome definitions differ. No arithmetic pooling or claim of universal loop effectiveness is justified.
- **Survey versus trained counterexample.** The 2024 TACL survey's strong conclusion is specifically about inference-time self-correction with prompted feedback, except favorable tasks, and it separately recognizes external feedback and fine-tuning. SCoRe is a later learned-policy intervention and therefore qualifies broader wording but does not refute the narrower conclusion. The survey also says strong comparable-cost baselines are often missing, leaving relative value unresolved.
- **Evaluator scores can improve while independent outcomes worsen.** Pan et al.'s essay study finds evaluator-human divergence; their separate constructed output/policy loops show proxy gains alongside toxicity/constraint violations. Both establish possibility in tested conditions, not frequency in deployed systems. Model-judge positional bias and annotator/task-formulation dependence add measurement uncertainty before the feedback is even optimized.
- **Round count and stopping.** Self-Debug reports one turn usually sufficed in its Spider setting; the reward-hacking experiments found adverse side effects detectable after multiple rounds in a constructed setting. These are not contradictory: one measures task accuracy, another omitted harm outcomes. Neither establishes a general optimal number of rounds or convergent stopping criterion. The survey's recommendation for comparable compute helps frame cost, but cost effects across loops remain underreported in these sources.
- **Feedback timing, sparsity and burden.** The reviewed cases include sparse response in an adaptive deployment and labeling/quality tradeoffs, but they do not permit a cross-domain estimate of how delay, sparsity, or human burden changes effectiveness. S1/S2 survey evidence suggests this is a material variable; no inference is made that all human review is burdensome or that every sparse-signal loop fails.
- **Contamination and evaluation reuse.** Li & Flanigan's work shows benchmark contamination can affect apparent performance, using several detection approaches. It does not prove contamination of Self-Refine, Self-Debug, or the other S2 benchmarks; such targeted audits were outside this subplan. In general, when feedback and evaluation reuse the same proxy, evaluator optimization can conceal construct drift, but that statement is analytic synthesis supported by E4/E5, not measured frequency.
- **Deployment causality and drift.** S2's clinical retrospective evidence finds longitudinal fragility, while governance sources prescribe monitoring. Neither tests causal outcomes of monitor-alert-remediate-retest. Adaptive bandit evidence is setting-specific, includes simulated tradeoffs and a low-response deployment; documentation of an operating process is not efficacy evidence.
- **Study-level quality/independence.** S1 is a literature survey and includes studies also referenced in S2; those citations do not create independent corroboration. The S2 case records are carried as prior reviewed evidence. New primary studies use different setups. Author/institution overlap across the broad literature was not systematically mapped. One source (S6) is assessed from its anthology record/abstract; S7 is assessed from its proceedings abstract here. Claims dependent on their omitted full details are narrowed accordingly.
- **Coverage boundary.** English-language discovery prioritized evaluation design, self-correction, evaluator reliability, signal provenance, proxy outcomes, leakage, and deployed monitoring because they cut across S1 mechanism families. It is not exhaustive of RLHF, recommender, retrieval, clinical, or agent evaluation; does not estimate effect sizes, costs, or failure prevalence; and makes no local workflow recommendations.

## Validation

- Positive, null, mixed, and adverse findings were retained. In particular, no-gain cases, feedback-following errors, diminishing marginal iterations, weak baselines, label disagreement, evaluator position bias, proxy-related harms, and absent causal monitoring evidence are visible alongside benchmark/deployment gains.
- The principal conflict/refutation path compared the 2024 TACL survey's prompted-feedback scope against 2024 SCoRe trained-RL results, distinguished both from tool-verified examples, and checked proxy scores against human/side-effect measures. Benchmark contamination evidence is treated as a threat to inference, not evidence that any named S2 study is contaminated.
- **Stopping rationale:** The prioritized cross-cutting questions now have evidence from a critical literature survey and primary trained self-correction, proxy-optimization, evaluator-bias, label-disagreement, and benchmark-contamination studies, complemented by the approved S1/S2 primary, observational and guidance records. The important alternatives have been checked: positive gains, no-gain/marginal cases, proxy harms, evaluator disagreement, trained-method counterevidence and measurement threats. Remaining gaps (independent deployment evidence, cross-domain costs, feedback delay/sparsity effects and loop-specific contamination audits) are stated and would need narrower, mechanism-specific searches to resolve; further broad search is unlikely to change these bounded conclusions. This is a reasoned stopping boundary, not a claim of literature completeness.
