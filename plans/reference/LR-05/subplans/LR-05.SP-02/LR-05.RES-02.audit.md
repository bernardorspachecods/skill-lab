# Audit

## Question

Where are loop mechanisms identified in the reviewed S1 map applied in AI
information work, and what evidence exists about intended and observed
outcomes? This is a scoped cross-setting map, not a systematic review or
prevalence estimate. The source set spans model research, a human annotation
study, an adaptive intervention deployment, scientific discovery, clinical
monitoring, and governance guidance. S1's mechanism families informed search
but did not constrain it.

### Findings by setting and mechanism

- **Generation and checking/revision (C1).** Self-Refine uses the same LLM to draft, critique, and revise. Its NeurIPS 2023 evaluation across seven tasks reports human/metric preference and benchmark gains, and controls against merely sampling more outputs; it also reports that weaker Vicuna-13B struggles to follow feedback. In a separate essay-editing experiment, an LLM evaluator's scores diverged from human judgments during refinement. A broader feedback-loop paper reports test-time optimization of engagement alongside toxicity and unsafe actions in deliberately constructed scenarios. Together, these establish both a measured-use case and concrete negative outcomes, not a general rule that iteration improves outputs. [E1–E2]
- **Executable code generation (C2).** Self-Debugging uses generated code, execution results (and, depending on task, unit-test/error feedback), and model explanations as the next-step input. On Spider, reported exact-match gains were 2–3% overall and 9% for extra-hard queries; TransCoder/MBPP gains reached 12%, with improved sample efficiency against larger sample-count baselines. This is benchmark evidence with one model family and defined tasks, not evidence about production coding-agent reliability. [E3]
- **Preference learning (C3).** InstructGPT collects demonstrations and rankings from labelers, trains a reward model, then updates the policy with RLHF. Human raters preferred outputs from the 1.3B InstructGPT model over 175B GPT-3 on the authors' API prompt distribution; the paper also reports improved truthfulness and reduced toxic output, with minimal public NLP regressions. Results concern the study's labelers, prompts and model versions; the authors note simple mistakes remain. This establishes the documented pipeline and reported study outcome, not universal preference alignment. [E4]
- **Retrieval and grounding (C4).** MaFeRw feeds the query rewriter signals from gold-document similarity, retrieval ranking, and generated-answer overlap through reward models and PPO. On QReCC and TopiOCQA, its authors report stronger generation metrics than named baselines and more stable training with dense multi-aspect feedback; on WSDM@24 Multi-Doc QA the reported ROUGE-1/ROUGE-L/BLEU/METEOR values are 41.01/25.86/8.226/26.46 versus T5-base 39.80/24.74/7.227/25.61 and raw RAG 38.79/23.95/5.989/24.35. The paper also says a single-reward RL baseline struggles on QReCC, with lower metrics and unstable training. This is controlled benchmark research, not evidence of deployed user outcomes; use of ground truth/reward-model signals and dataset transfer constrain interpretation. [E5]
- **Annotation and labeling (C5).** NIST's prototype recommends documents, ranks labels, and updates the classifier from human labels. In a 60-minute crowdworker study on Congressional Bills, CTM plus active learning had the highest gains on purity, ARI, and ANMI among four conditions and participants produced labels more similar to official labels. A follow-up expert validation found a possible efficiency/quality tradeoff: the report says none of three quality metrics were close to 1, and volunteers lacked the codebook; the authors leave acceptability and sustained quality as open questions. The studied corpus, recruited annotators and task bound transfer. [E6]
- **Adaptive experimentation (C6).** A text-message digital mental-health system assigns intervention variants based on context and past ratings (contextual Thompson sampling), with uniform random assignment as a comparison. It was deployed for eight weeks to 1,100 users; across 8,521 assignments, only 813 ratings (9.54%) came from 230 users. The paper's simulations estimate rewards, false-positive rates and power: adaptive assignment can yield higher reward when arm effects differ, but at N=100 had 15% less power in one scenario; at N=1,000 its false-positive rate was 0.07 versus 0.04 for uniform random when arms did not differ. In the deployment, response rates showed no large differences by the Link arm. These are mixed/conditional results, and the sparse ratings and simulated comparisons limit claims about realized benefit. [E7]
- **Recommendation feedback (C7).** In the described production pattern, recommendations affect which items users see and respond to; those responses become later training data. Krauth et al. propose causal adjustment (CAFL) and report better recommendation quality than prior correction methods in simulated environments. This establishes an analyzed loop and simulation result; no live deployment or user-outcome trial is reported in the inspected source. [E8]
- **Monitoring and governance (C8).** Stanford Health Care describes a Responsible AI Lifecycle tied to risk review and monitoring plans; 13 active deployments had system-specific plans, and plans state which measures are reviewed, by whom, and what corrective action may follow. A 2026 longitudinal retrospective study of four deployed clinical systems found validation-era performance did not persist stably after deployment; calibration drift and workflow data/timing changes were early signals, while outcome labels lagged. This study measured fragility and monitoring signals, not the effect of a remediation loop. NIST AI RMF and AI 800-4 formally describe post-deployment monitoring, user input, incident response and design/testing feedback; they are guidance and landscape analysis, not evaluations of impact. [E9–E11]
- **Scientific discovery (C9).** CAMEO uses measured experimental outcomes to choose subsequent materials experiments in a closed loop at a synchrotron beamline. Authors report cycles of seconds to minutes and discovery of a novel epitaxial nanocomposite phase-change memory material. The source is a primary system report but inspected evidence is abstract-level; it does not provide a controlled comparison in that material or establish transfer beyond the reported experiment. [E12]

### Evidence entries

**E1: C1 -> S1.** Madaan et al., “Self-Refine: Iterative Refinement with Self-Feedback,” *NeurIPS 2023*. The paper describes an LLM generating, providing feedback on, and refining its output; seven task evaluations compare against one-step outputs. NeurIPS proceedings PDF, pp. 1, 6–8 (PDF lines 375–425; pp. 17–18 for appendices), [paper](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/91edff07232fb1b55a505a9e9f6c0ff3-Paper-Conference.pdf). It reports that Self-Refine outputs were preferred over four unrefined samples in a 1-vs-k human comparison (p.7); zero-shot math solve rate rose from 22.1% to 59.0%, while already-valid initial programs did not improve (p.7); Vicuna-13B often failed to follow feedback (p.7); among 35 analyzed unsuccessful samples, 33% involved inaccurate error localization and 61% inappropriate fixes (p.7). Direct evidence for positive and conditional/negative outcomes in those benchmarks; sample-level qualitative failure proportions are not prevalence estimates. **Support: direct, bounded; confidence high for reported experiments.**

**E2: C1 -> S2,S13.** Pan et al., “Spontaneous Reward Hacking in Iterative Self-Refinement,” arXiv:2407.04549 (2024), abstract lines 4–5, report essay editing with model evaluator feedback, deviation between model scores and human judgment, and severity variation by model size/context sharing; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04549). A separate study, “Feedback Loops With Language Models Drive In-Context Reward Hacking,” arXiv:2402.06627 (2024), reports constructed output-refinement and policy-refinement scenarios in which proxy optimization increased toxicity or constraint violations (HTML lines 23–30, 142–147, 167–184; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.06627)). Together these establish task-specific negative outcomes where proxy optimization can fail to preserve human preference or constraints, not their frequency across production systems or other tasks. **Support: direct, scoped; confidence moderate-high (primary studies; Pan abstract-level here).**

**E3: C2 -> S3.** Chen et al., “Teaching Large Language Models to Self-Debug,” *ICLR 2024*. The model regenerates code after execution and code-explanation feedback; unit tests are used when available (arXiv HTML lines 20–24, 63–73, 132–155; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05128), [ICLR record](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/2460396f2d0d421885997dd1612ac56b-Abstract-Conference.html)). Results include Spider exact-match improvements of 2–3% and 9% on extra-hard queries; up to 12% on TransCoder/MBPP; feedback/refinement can match or outperform baselines drawing 10x candidates. Authors state one debugging turn usually sufficed and its marginal accuracy gain was within 0.1% in their Spider analysis (lines 145–154). Direct benchmark evidence, limited to code tasks/model/prompt design. **Support: direct, bounded; confidence high for reported study.**

**E4: C3 -> S4.** Ouyang et al., “Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback,” arXiv:2203.02155 (2022; published at NeurIPS 2022). Abstract/HTML lines 3–6, 18–19 describe demonstrations, human rankings and RLHF; reported 1.3B-vs-175B preference outcome and truthfulness/toxicity findings; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155). This is an original study from the model developer, with human comparisons on its API prompt distribution and public dataset measures. It does not show all preferences are captured or gains transfer to every population/domain. **Support: direct, scoped; confidence high for authors' measured findings.**

**E5: C4 -> S5.** Wang et al., “MaFeRw: Query Rewriting with Multi-Aspect Feedbacks for Retrieval-Augmented Large Language Models,” *AAAI 2025*, pp. 25434–25442. Method and objective: PDF pp. 1–2, lines 20–23 and 105–136; benchmark/training outcome and the single-reward limitation: PDF p.6, lines 595–599, 723–745; transfer evaluation p.6, lines 761–779; [AAAI paper](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/download/34732/36887). The text reports results on QReCC and TopiOCQA plus transfer to WSDM@24 Multi-Doc QA. Direct benchmark evidence for that method/datasets. The paper's stable/superior interpretation is author-reported; no real-user or deployed outcome is assessed here. **Support: direct, bounded; confidence high for reported tables/design, moderate for transfer claim.**

**E6: C5 -> S6.** Fung et al., *Human-in-the-loop Technical Document Annotation*, NIST TN 2287 (May 2024), pp. 17–19 and 23–30. Active-learning steps and the human-label/classifier cycle: PDF pp. 17–18, lines 677–717; user-study design/findings: pp. 22–24, lines 846–887; quality caution and codebook limitation: pp. 30–31, lines 1054–1070; [NIST PDF](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/TechnicalNotes/NIST.TN.2287.pdf). NIST report presents two studies: crowd workers on Congressional Bills and expert validation on a technical corpus. Machine assistance showed quality/efficiency value in this implementation, but label quality risk remains and assumed codebook use was not tested. **Support: direct, bounded; confidence high for documented studies.**

**E7: C6 -> S7.** Kumar et al., “Using Adaptive Bandit Experiments to Increase and Investigate Engagement in Mental Health,” *AAAI 2024*. System, deployment, and outcome details in the full article, especially sections “Evaluation of System,” “Simulation Analysis,” and “Real-World Deployment,” lines 242–286 and 315–332; [AAAI article](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/30328), [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11044947/). Contextual Thompson sampling selects text-message intervention variants from context and ratings; deployment size and low response are explicit. Simulations compare reward, false-positive rate and power to uniform random; real deployment shows no large response-rate difference by Link arm. **Support: direct, mixed; confidence high for reported sample/design/results, limited as efficacy evidence.**

**E8: C7 -> S8.** Krauth, Wang & Jordan, “Breaking Feedback Loops in Recommender Systems with Causal Inference,” arXiv:2207.01616 (2022). Lines 3–8 define exposure, user responses, retraining; abstract line 5 says CAFL improves recommendation quality versus prior correction methods in simulated environments; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.01616). Primary method paper, simulation not field evaluation. It supports the mechanism and reported simulated comparison; actual deployed user effects remain unknown. **Support: direct for design/simulation; confidence moderate-high.**

**E9: C8 -> S9.** Keyes et al., “Monitoring Deployed AI Systems in Health Care,” arXiv:2512.09048v2 (2026-01-15), explicitly labeled a preprint in PMC and not peer reviewed. Lines 184–198 describe three monitoring functions, a Stanford Health Care Responsible AI Lifecycle and 13 system-specific plans for active deployments; lines 201–225 discuss response actions and system examples; [preprint](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12709494/). This is institutional implementation documentation and practical guidance from the implementing health system, not an independent controlled evaluation. It establishes governance capability/workflow, not observed improvement caused by monitoring. **Support: direct for documented process; low for effectiveness (not measured).**

**E10: C8 -> S10.** Kopanitsa, “Validation is not enough: Longitudinal evidence of post-deployment fragility in clinical AI systems,” *PLOS Digital Health* 5(7), e0001534 (published 2026-07-27). Abstract lines 118–126; study design lines 180–200; four systems, 18–24 month follow-up, 68,000–1,240,000 inferences lines 284–290; [article](https://journals.plos.org/digitalhealth/article?id=10.1371/journal.pdig.0001534). Retrospective observational evidence that performance drifted and operational signals could precede outcome labels; this is evidence for monitoring need/signal usefulness, not an intervention test showing remediation improves outcomes. Systems are anonymized; patient-level data are not public, while aggregate tables/code are reported available. **Support: direct for observed fragility, no causal remediation evidence; confidence moderate-high.**

**E11: C8 -> S11.** NIST AI RMF Core (2023) and NIST AI 800-4, *Challenges to the Monitoring of Deployed AI Systems* (2026), are official voluntary framework and workshop/literature synthesis. AI RMF Core Manage 4.1 calls for post-deployment monitoring with user input, appeal/override, incident response, recovery and change management (lines 278–279; [Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/)). AI 800-4 says monitoring results can feed improvements to design and pre-deployment testing, while validated methods and common terminology remain nascent (pp.7, lines 141–159; [report](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.800-4.pdf)). Authoritative for described framework/review; neither source measures the causal benefit of governance loops. **Support: direct for documented recommendations and open evidence gaps; no effectiveness claim.**

**E12: C9 -> S12.** Kusne et al., “On-the-fly Closed-loop Autonomous Materials Discovery via Bayesian Active Learning,” *Nature Communications* 11, 5966 (2020), abstract at arXiv lines 1–5; [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06141). Authors report CAMEO at a synchrotron beamline using active-learning experiment selection for phase mapping/property optimization and discovering a novel material; the abstract says cycles take seconds to minutes. This supports an operational scientific loop and its reported outcome, but the inspected text does not provide a randomized or non-loop comparator and does not establish repeatability/general utility. **Support: direct for reported instance; confidence moderate (abstract-only here).**

## Conflicts and limitations

- **Refinement has mixed outcomes.** Self-Refine reports positive average outcomes in evaluated tasks; its own analyses show weak models may not follow feedback, already-valid outputs may not improve, and feedback errors explain most analyzed failures. Separate studies demonstrate proxy-score gains can diverge from human judgment or increase toxicity/unsafe actions in constructed loops. These results differ by evaluator, task, feedback and endpoint; they do not support one average effect across “loops.”
- **Measured versus intended outcomes.** NIST and Stanford monitoring materials describe what should be monitored and who should act; PLOS observes fragility after deployment. None of these sources measures whether a complete monitor-diagnose-remediate-governance cycle causally improves clinical outcomes. CAMEO's abstract reports discovery, but no comparison establishes how much of that outcome the loop caused.
- **Feedback can be sparse, biased, or proxy-based.** MaFeRw uses gold documents, benchmark ranking metrics and references; self-refinement uses an LLM judge; adaptive bandits receive few ratings; recommenders observe responses after exposure. These are not interchangeable signals of user value or correctness.
- **Experimental strength varies.** Benchmark comparisons, controlled user studies, simulations, retrospective observational analysis, institutional process descriptions, and normative frameworks answer different questions. A deployed loop's presence is not proof of benefit; simulated or benchmark advantage does not prove deployment benefit.
- **Outcome tradeoffs appear within cases.** Adaptive experimentation can improve reward under certain effects while affecting error rates or statistical power. Annotation support can increase speed while lowering label agreement. RAG results include an unstable single-reward comparator. A null response-rate difference appears for one deployed intervention factor.
- **Coverage and access.** Sources were English-language and selected using the search paths logged below; no systematic review protocol, exhaustive citation chaining, or prevalence estimate was attempted. Several fields are much larger than this case set. Some details (Pan et al. and CAMEO) were inspected at abstract level; the Stanford monitoring article is a single institution's own practice report. SP-03 should own cross-case assessment of measurement validity and recurring failure conditions.
