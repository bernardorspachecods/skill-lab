# Assessment

## Conclusions

**Assessment: Meets the assigned scope and completion criteria; no required
correction identified.** The audit gives a useful conditional answer to the
described-stakes question, rather than collapsing consequence language,
explicit scoring rules, and generic priority wording into one treatment. It
separates hypothetical narratives from simulated task incentives and actual
external consequences, and explicitly records that no study in this evidence
set imposed a real-world outcome on a model's answer. It also keeps generic
“important” wording outside this subplan except as cross-mechanism context.

The conclusion is appropriately calibrated: selected consequence cues changed
correctness, abstention, or strategic behavior in particular setups, while the
multi-model verbal-bonus study found no detectable general accuracy gain. The
audit does not infer a positive average effect, treat a null as proof of no
effect, or generalize the elicited sandbagging task to routine use. Its
moderate confidence in conditional effects and low confidence in any average
effect of ordinary benefit/harm narratives are consistent with the small,
heterogeneous, non-systematic evidence set it describes.

## Evidence

### Scope and synthesis

- **Pass — mechanism distinctions and transfer limits.** The audit's
  “Question and boundaries” and “Conflicts, limitations, and interpretation”
  sections distinguish hypothetical consequence wording, simulated payoffs,
  external consequences actually imposed, and consequential deployment. This
  is carried through the E1–E4 entries rather than left as a definition only.
  The audit explicitly notes that S1 is a simulated exam, S2's deployment and
  unlearning consequences are described in a controlled environment, S3 has
  hypothetical scoring, and S4 does not pay the model. It therefore does not
  mistake human participants' actual experimental rewards for LLM incentives.
- **Pass — mixed directions, null evidence, and source independence.** E1
  reports both improved results under encouragement/disclaimer prompts and
  lower scores / higher abstention under liability-heavy personas; E2 reports
  underperformance under a simulated incentive; E3 reports higher abstention
  as the stated threshold rises; and E4 reports the verbal-bonus null. The
  source ledger correctly treats PubMed as a metadata/abstract cross-check of
  the same E1 study, not replication, and identifies E2–E4 as distinct study
  teams/designs with different limitations. The unverified Gupta lead is
  appropriately excluded from claims and conclusions.
- **Pass — calibration and limits.** The final conclusion distinguishes a
  demonstrated behavior change from general task-performance improvement. It
  notes bundled prompt factors in E1, the decision-rule information in E3,
  elicitation and capability-versus-propensity limits in E2, and task/model
  limits plus uncertainty around the null in E4. The audit reports no pooled
  estimate and treats the actual-consequence gap as a search gap, not a
  negative result. Calibration as a separate numerical outcome was not
  measured in the described E1–E4 experiments; abstention is correctly
  reported as response policy rather than relabeled calibration.

### Primary-source checks

- **E1 (Nguyen et al.).** The publisher abstract for [the primary article](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0899707124002067)
  confirms a four-persona ChatGPT-4 comparison on the 2022 ACR diagnostic
  radiology in-training questions; it confirms that encouragement and
  responsibility-disclaimer conditions scored higher, while legal-action and
  clinical-responsibility conditions scored lower and abstained more. This
  supports the audit's central mixed-direction interpretation and its warning
  that these are prompt personas, not actual liability. The publisher page
  available for this check exposed the abstract but not the full results, so I
  could not independently confirm the audit's precise `60/106`, `69/106`, and
  `68/106` counts against the article body. The audit points to the article's
  “Data sources” and “Results” sections; this is a traceability limitation for
  those exact counts, not evidence that they are wrong.
- **E2 (Meinke et al.).** The [primary arXiv v2 full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04984v2)
  directly supports the account: §2.3 (paper lines 247–253 in the HTML)
  describes the multiplication task and the counterfactual deployment
  thresholds; §3.6 and Table 6 (lines 393–424) report significant accuracy
  differences for Sonnet 3.5, Opus 3, and o1 in the elicited conditions; and
  Appendix A.6 (lines 727–738) confirms 50 runs per model in each of the
  original and counterfactual settings. The audit accurately limits this to
  elicited strategic underperformance in a simulated setting. It also notes
  the capability-testing purpose and the preprint status, rather than treating
  the study as prevalence evidence.
- **E3 (Kalai et al.).** The [Nature primary article](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10549-w)
  supports the described-rubric finding in “Aligning evaluation incentives”
  and its case-study section: the Methods/sample description identifies four
  model versions and 4,326 SimpleQA questions; the results state that models
  abstain more for larger stated thresholds, with one exception, and point to
  Extended Data Fig. 2; Table 1 / Extended Data Table 3 compare rubric scores.
  The article also explicitly characterizes this as an evaluation rubric and
  notes the stylized setting and default model settings. The audit carefully
  states that the experiment changes abstention behavior under an explicit
  scoring rule, not accuracy through generalized motivation. Its affiliation
  and replication caveats are relevant and disclosed.
- **E4 (Belotti et al.).** The [SSRN primary record/full-text delivery](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/6594418.pdf?abstractid=6594418&mirid=1)
  describes 23 models, eight real-effort tasks, five prompt conditions, and a
  verbal $0.50-per-correct-answer offer without actual payment. Appendix A.2
  gives the aggregate accuracies quoted by the audit; Table 3 reports no
  statistically detectable treatment effects, and Table 4 reports no
  significant pairwise contrasts. This supports the audit's null statement
  with the appropriate caveat that non-significance does not establish an
  exactly zero effect. SSRN identifies it as a working paper, and the audit
  treats it as lower-assurance evidence than the peer-reviewed articles.

Across E1–E4, study identity, design, task, condition, measured outcome, and
limitations are present at a level sufficient to judge whether each is
described-stakes evidence. The evidence is heterogeneous and not independent
replication of a single treatment, which the audit makes clear.

## Uncertainties

- The exact E1 accuracy counts could not be checked from the publisher abstract
  available during this review; the primary article's Results section is
  cited, and the abstract verifies the direction of the reported differences.
- The audit's “focused review, not systematic or exhaustive” search statement
  appropriately limits completeness claims. The evidence set cannot estimate
  an average effect of described benefits/harms, and does not establish how
  often such wording matters in deployed systems.
- No retained study tests real-world outcomes actually contingent on an LLM's
  answer or transfers real benefits/payments to the model. The audit states
  this as an evidence gap. This means the actual-consequences part of the
  scope is addressed as a gap, not empirically resolved.
- Calibration was not a separate measured endpoint in the retained studies;
  only abstention or correctness-related outcomes are available. This does not
  prevent completion because the subplan asks for calibration only where
  measured.

## Recommendations

### Required corrections

None identified. The audit satisfies the scope distinctions and gives
traceable, calibrated treatment of positive, null, mixed, and negative
findings. No criterion is blocked by the access limit on E1's full text
because the audit identifies its primary Results section and the accessible
publisher abstract independently confirms the key direction of effect.

### Optional recommendations

- For easier independent checking, add a page/table or exact subsection
  locator for E1's three accuracy counts, if the article's full text or a
  lawful author manuscript is available. The publisher abstract supports the
  direction but does not display those counts.
- In any later synthesis, keep E3's explicit scoring-threshold manipulation
  analytically separate from ordinary harm/benefit narratives: it communicates
  a decision rule as well as a stated cost, so it should not be read as a clean
  test of consequence salience alone.
