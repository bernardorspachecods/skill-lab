# Audit

## Question

What terms and mechanisms describe feedback loops in direct user–AI workflows for information work (researching, planning, drafting, analyzing, coding, and tool use)? Which findings are direct evidence about a person working with an AI system, and which describe adjacent agent-internal or model-training loops? This is a bounded terminology/mechanism map, not an exhaustive review or a claim that any one loop design is universally effective.

## Claims and evidence

| ID | Material claim / question | Importance | Evidence expected | Linked evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | There is no single stable label for the user-facing phenomenon. Studies use workflow, interaction, collaboration, prompt iteration, monitoring/evaluation, confirmation, and domain-process terms (e.g., information-foraging and sensemaking loops). | High: vocabulary determines what evidence a later search finds. | Primary user studies and explicit workflow definitions. | E1–E4, E6, E8–E9 | Supported, bounded corpus |
| C2 | In direct user–AI work, a recurring outer loop is: express/update an intent or task; receive an AI response/action/result; inspect it against goals or evidence; then accept/use, reject/edit, clarify/re-prompt, redirect, verify, or stop. | High: core mechanism map. | Observed interaction traces, study findings, or explicitly documented user controls. | E1–E7, E9 | Supported across distinct task contexts; individual steps vary |
| C3 | In multi-step agents, user oversight may be checkpointed: inspect progress/state, detect an error, diagnose where it began, correct the instruction/state, and redo dependent steps. Confirmation placement trades user inspection overhead against error propagation/rework in the studied tasks. | High: agent-specific terminology and operational mechanism. | Agent-task study, explicit sequence, comparison/design evaluation. | E5 | Supported in one controlled study; transfer limits are material |
| C4 | The feedback signal and verification method depend on the work: semantic relevance/clarity in information seeking; evidence traceability and direction in research; execution/static analysis/docs in coding; hypothesis/data checks in analysis; requirement/progress checks in multi-step tool tasks; learner evaluation/revision in writing. | High: prevents treating “feedback” as one undifferentiated signal. | Primary studies with examples and methods. | E2–E7, E9 | Supported as a cross-study grouping; not a tested universal taxonomy |
| C5 | Some agent research uses “loop” for an agent-internal action–observation or reflection cycle. This is adjacent evidence: it explains how an agent updates its own plan, but by itself does not show how a human user should prompt, supervise, or verify it. Model-training feedback is another distinct meaning and is outside this S1 evidence set. | High: avoid conflating technical meanings with workflow practice. | Original agent-method definition and explicit scope distinction. | E8 | Agent-internal distinction supported; training literature not reviewed here |
| C6 | Evidence that a loop mechanism “works” must be scoped to the outcome actually measured. Observational and qualitative studies identify observed strategies and associations; simulation/controlled studies estimate effects only under their task/interface conditions. ### Evidence records

**E1: C1, C2, C4, C6 → S1 — developer–SWE-agent collaboration in open issues.** Kumar et al., *Why AI Agents Still Need You: Findings from Developer-Agent Collaborations in the Wild*, arXiv:2506.12347v3 (2025-10-09), accepted at ASE 2025; inspected HTML, https://arxiv.org/html/2506.12347v3. §III-C–D, lines 193–203: 19 professional developers, study sessions up to 60 minutes, in-IDE Cursor agent, issue work, prompts and action trajectories; participants were asked whether a final patch was satisfactory and to continue until satisfied where possible. §IV-A1, lines 245–260: authors identify one-shot delegation versus incremental resolution, where a participant decomposes an issue into sequential subtasks and prompts the agent in separate turns; strategies can combine. §IV-C / Table II, lines 347–362: success associations: 38% for one-shot and 83% for incremental resolution; requested code refinements associated with 68% vs 30% without them. §V-C, lines 390–394: authors caution that the agent often agreed with the latest user correction rather than engaging in substantive problem-solving; a participant who invited challenge surfaced an additional issue. §III-F–G, lines 225–238: exploratory descriptive analysis, no inferential statistics for these patterns; one-company sample, early use, observer and forced-use effects, and tool/model/time limits. **Relation:** direct observations support terminology and mechanisms; reported percentages are descriptive associations, not causal proof that incremental prompting produces success. **What this establishes:** a direct agent workflow can include task decomposition, iterative prompts/refinements, execution monitoring, and user acceptance of a final patch; user correction can steer the agent, but compliance alone may not add independent reasoning. **Does not establish:** a best prompt cadence or generalizable effect across IDEs, domains, long-term use, or non-company settings. **Status:** direct partial support for C1/C2/C4; strong limit evidence for C6. Confidence: moderate for observed mechanism, low-to-moderate for comparative association.

**E2: C1, C2, C4, C6 → S2 — human–AI information seeking through interactive deep research.** Pan et al., *InterDeepResearch: Enabling Human-Agent Collaborative Information Seeking through Interactive Deep Research*, arXiv:2603.12608v1 (2026-03-13), preprint; inspected HTML, https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12608v1. §3.2, lines 130–137: formative findings describe incomplete observability of research details/dependencies and users’ desire for timely steering; existing systems often restrict users to an initial query and final report. §5.2 and context backtrace, lines 224–225: user selects generated content and triggers a source-evidence trace through action dependencies to raw information. §8.1–8.3, lines 278–300: 15 frequent deep-research users received system training, did tasks, then rated views/interactions and discussed them; a research-action dependency graph was rated 4.7/5, while cross-view linking had mixed feedback. §8.3.5 and discussion, lines 320–324: authors characterize the results as an early signal and state that user preferences/usage patterns differ across scenarios. **Relation:** supports direct steering/inspection and evidence-tracing mechanisms; ratings are perceptions/usability evidence, not improved report accuracy or task outcomes. **What this establishes:** information-seeking loops can include steering research while it is running, observing action dependencies, and tracing claims back to evidence. **Does not establish:** comparative performance or causal benefits; a small formative/preprint study cannot establish broad user preference. **Status:** direct support for C1/C2/C4 and limitation for C6. Confidence: moderate for documented mechanisms, low for efficacy.

**E3: C1, C2, C4, C6 → S3 — generative AI-assisted data analysis and sensemaking.** Drosos et al., *“It’s like a rubber duck that talks back”: Understanding Generative AI-Assisted Data Analysis Workflows through a Participatory Prompting Study*, CHIWORK 2024, DOI 10.1145/3663384.3663389; inspected publisher-hosted paper PDF, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/drosos_2024_bing_participatory_prompting.pdf. Abstract and §1, PDF pp. 0–1, lines 33–54 and 112–128: N=15 Bing Chat study; authors report assistance with information foraging and sensemaking, with barriers in query formulation, context provision, and verification. §2.1, p. 1, lines 133–141: the paper defines data analysis sensemaking as two iterative processes—information foraging and hypothesis development/testing. §3.1 and Figure 1, pp. 3–4, lines 315–338 and p. 0, lines 29–32: researcher-mediated participatory prompting alternates participant intent, prompt formulation, AI response, and participant reflection/next intent. §3.4, p. 5, lines 463–502: participant tasks, researcher-operated Bing Chat, think-aloud response assessment, and follow-up/pivot choices. §4.1, pp. 6–7, lines 594–608: examples of filtering/searching, hypothesis/analysis planning, and iteration. §6, p. 18, lines 1617–1645: conclusion and explicit limitations: researcher mediation reduces external validity and can influence workflows; observed verification requires manual source checking and hallucination checking. **Relation:** direct evidence of a user-facing AI workflow, although mediated by a researcher; reported workflow loops are domain-process loops and not necessarily autonomous-agent loops. **What this establishes:** analysis work may alternate searching/filtering with hypothesis generation/testing, with AI assisting and the user evaluating and revising goals/prompts. **Does not establish:** how participants would behave unaided in real work or a causal productivity/accuracy effect. **Status:** supports C1/C2/C4/C6. Confidence: moderate for mechanisms, low for unmediated transfer.

**E4: C1, C2, C4, C6 → S4 — health information seeking with conversational AI (bounded adjacent high-stakes domain).** Ramesh et al., *Metacognitive Demands and Strategies While Using Off-The-Shelf AI Conversational Agents for Health Information Seeking*, CHI 2026 paper/arXiv:2602.05111v1; inspected HTML, https://arxiv.org/html/2602.05111v1. §3, lines 64–66: think-aloud study with 15 participants using a ChatGPT-4o API interface and two of six fictional health scenarios per participant. §4.3, lines 201–206: authors define prompt iteration as refining/rephrasing a query based on the previous response; participants experimented with wording when the response missed the question or the agent did not clarify what it misunderstood. §4 results and Table 1, lines 147–166: workflow coding included formulation, evaluation, iteration, understanding/adapting; study is qualitative. §5 discussion, lines 243–250: authors identify risks of unproductive rewording/agent anchoring and describe knowing when to redirect or disengage as workflow demands. §5.2 limitations, lines 291–294: study aims for depth rather than statistical generalization; sample size/context constrain transfer. **Relation:** directly studies user-facing information seeking, but it is simulated health scenarios and not autonomous tool-using agent work. **What this establishes:** “prompt iteration,” “evaluation,” and “workflow adaptation” are observed/user-coded stages; rephrasing can itself become an unproductive loop where the system remains anchored. **Does not establish:** effectiveness in actual health decisions or general information seeking. **Status:** supports C1/C2/C4 and an adverse case for C6. Confidence: moderate for reported qualitative mechanics, low for external generalization.

**E5: C2, C3, C4, C6 → S5 — checkpointing and repair in multi-step agent tasks.** Zhou et al., *When Should Users Check? Modeling Confirmation Frequency in Multi-Step Agentic AI Tasks*, CHI 2026, DOI 10.1145/3772318.3790655; arXiv:2510.05307v4 (2026-09-15), https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05307v4. Abstract, lines 95–99: formative study with eight people identified a Confirmation–Diagnosis–Correction–Redo (CDCR) pattern; within-subjects evaluation with 48 participants reported 81% preference for intermediate confirmations and 13.54% lower completion time than confirm-at-end. §3.2, lines 180–205: formative study used five agent-task cases, think-aloud, and interviews; authors say participants reviewed execution history to locate errors. §6.1–6.3, lines 355–371: “verification” experiment used a simulated environment with fixed agent accuracy/time and three task domains (shopping, image editing, Overcooked), compared model-scheduled intermediate confirmation with end-only confirmation. §5.3, lines 338–345: at a checkpoint, if a user finds the state wrong, CDCR may lead back to an earlier state; changed action sequence can require recomputation. §8–9, lines 446–450: authors frame results as a mixed-initiative design opportunity; the abstract/conclusion report the measured preference/time outcomes. **Relation:** direct human supervision of simulated/real agent task histories, but not a live deployment and not knowledge-work outcome testing. **What this establishes:** the CDCR label and user repair sequence are explicit; in this study’s simulated tasks, model-scheduled checkpoints beat the confirm-at-end baseline on completion time and were preferred. **Does not establish:** universal checkpoint frequency, effects in real office/research/coding tasks, or accuracy/safety outcomes; simulation isolates confirmation cadence. **Status:** direct support for C2/C3 and limited efficacy evidence for C6. Confidence: moderate for the mechanism; low-to-moderate for transfer beyond study tasks.

**E6: C1, C2, C4, C6 → S6 — code-generating model interaction (assistant, not autonomous agent).** Barke, James & Polikarpova, *Grounded Copilot: How Programmers Interact with Code-Generating Models*, PACMPL 7, OOPSLA1 (2023), pp. 85–111, DOI 10.1145/3586030; author manuscript HTML, https://arxiv.org/html/2206.15000v3. §3, lines 132–139 (participants/method); §4.1–4.2, lines 161–196 and 201–260: authors distinguish “acceleration” (known next step; quick pattern-matching of suggestions) from “exploration” (uncertainty about APIs/algorithms; deliberate natural-language prompts, multiple suggestions, careful validation). §4.2.5, lines 245–253: validation examples include line-by-line examination, execution/tests/REPL, static analysis/type checking, and consulting documentation. §5.1.2–5.1.3, lines 298–313: intentional comment prompting/multiple suggestions were more common in exploration; validation mix differed by mode and task. §8, lines 386–390: limitations include designed code-authorship tasks, no normal project context/time pressure, sample skewed toward academia, and short exposure. **Relation:** direct person–AI interaction but a code completion model rather than agent executing tools. **What this establishes:** different confidence/task conditions corresponded to different prompting and verification tactics in this study. **Does not establish:** those tactics improve outcomes or transfer to coding agents. **Status:** supports C1/C2/C4; limits C6. Confidence: moderate for observed categories, low for broader prescriptions.

**E7: C2, C4, C6 → S7 — direct SWE-agent use in professional coding.** Same S1 as E1; not an independent source or corroboration. §IV-B2, lines 326–332: potentially destructive terminal actions could change environment/codebase and disrupt a user; one participant abandoned a task after a long command sequence corrupted the environment. §IV-C / Table II, lines 347–362: agent strategy/action associations with success; §V-C, lines 390–394: discuss challenge vs uncritical agreement. **Relation:** adds concrete environment feedback and consequence checks to E1; shares the same sample and provenance cluster. **What this establishes:** a user-agent loop may include inspecting tool side effects, not only reviewing generated text/code. **Does not establish:** frequency/risk across other tools or a causal safety intervention. **Status:** supports C2/C4 and contributes limits to C6. Confidence: moderate for observed cases only.

**E8: C5 → S8 — agent-internal action–observation loop terminology.** Kumar et al., same source/version as S1, §II-A, lines 116–124, distinguishes an autonomous SWE agent’s ability to refine behavior from environment feedback from an in-IDE agent collaborating with a developer. ReAct and Reflexion were discovered in the research route (Q5) as likely original agent-method sources, but their full text was not independently opened/verified for this S1 artifact; they are therefore not cited as evidence here. **Relation:** direct source framing supports a distinction between agent action/environment feedback and human–agent collaboration. **What this establishes:** “agent loop” can name system-side refinement from environment feedback, distinct from user-facing prompting/review. **Does not establish:** the precise ReAct/Reflexion mechanisms from their primary papers or their user-workflow utility. **Status:** partial support; vocabulary boundary is cautious. Confidence: low-to-moderate.

**E9: C1, C2, C4, C6 → S9 — writing feedback/task-offloading sequences.** Lai et al., *What task to offload to GenAI in the writing feedback process? Effects of task-offloading approaches on EFL learners’ writing skill development*, *Computers and Education* 252 (Oct 2026), article 105675, DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2026.105675; inspected university repository publication record/abstract, https://research.cuhk.edu.hk/en/publications/what-task-to-offload-to-genai-in-the-writing-feedback-process-eff/. Abstract, lines 24–29: quasi-experimental comparison of 101 university EFL learners in three conditions: (1) GenAI drafts, learner evaluates and revises; (2) learner drafts, GenAI evaluates, learner revises; and (3) learner drafts/evaluates/revises. Repository abstract reports the first condition had the greatest writing gains and greater learning-oriented interaction, metacognitive outcomes, and self-efficacy. **Relation:** supports a task-role sequence and the importance of human evaluation/revision in writing; the abstract does not expose enough interaction procedure or detailed measures for deeper loop analysis. **What this establishes:** a recent quasi-experimental writing study directly compared who drafts, evaluates, and revises, and reported stronger learning outcomes in one arrangement. **Does not establish:** a general-purpose agent workflow, a multi-step tool-using agent, exact feedback-loop mechanics, or transfer beyond university EFL learning; full article was not accessible in this pass. **Status:** limited support for C1/C2/C4, useful outcome signal for C6 with source-access caveat. Confidence: low-to-moderate.

**Synthesis across rows:** the repeated outer operation can be summarized as intent/task → AI response/action → human or environment evidence → next action, but the signal and next-cycle change differ by task. This four-part description is my cross-source grouping, not a source-reported universal model. Direct cases have a human changing or evaluating the workflow; E6 and E9 are bounded adjacent assistance studies; E8 is system-level adjacent. Missing values remain explicit above rather than being inferred.

### Finding cards (academic orientation; E# remains canonical support)

**F1 — Coding-agent collaboration:** Participants used one-shot or incremental issue resolution; refinements and expert input co-occurred with higher descriptive success rates, but the design does not warrant causal attribution. Evidence: E1, E7.

**F2 — Information-seeking research:** Proposed direct interaction includes observing the ongoing research graph, steering its direction, and tracing generated conclusions to source evidence; available user evidence is early and primarily perception/usability ratings. Evidence: E2.

**F3 — Data analysis:** Information foraging and hypothesis/sensemaking are domain loops; AI can help query/search and suggest analyses, while the person revises intent and checks outputs. Researcher mediation helps elicit interaction but limits ecological transfer. Evidence: E3.

**F4 — Prompt iteration and adaptation:** User rephrasing, evaluation, redirecting, and stopping are observable workflow stages; repeated wording may not resolve a system’s anchored misunderstanding. Evidence: E4.

**F5 — Agent checkpoints:** Confirmation–Diagnosis–Correction–Redo names one observed recovery sequence. Intermediate confirmation improved measured time/preference in a simulated set of tasks relative to confirm-at-end; transfer is open. Evidence: E5.

**F6 — Code suggestion verification:** “Acceleration” and “exploration” are task/user interaction modes in a code-completion study; validation may rely on examination, running, static analysis, or documentation, not just another prompt. Evidence: E6.

**F7 — Writing task roles:** A quasi-experiment compared GenAI drafting with learner evaluation/revision against other task allocations; the repository abstract reports the first arrangement produced greater writing gains for its EFL learner sample. Full procedure/outcomes and non-educational transfer remain unverified here; the system is not an autonomous tool-using agent. Evidence: E9.

## Conflicts and limitations

- **Terminology overlap, not contradiction:** “loop” can refer to a domain work process (information foraging/sensemaking), a user–AI turn-taking/iteration pattern, an oversight/recovery sequence (CDCR), or an agent’s environment-feedback cycle. These are nested or adjacent constructs, not synonyms. The source set does not support a canonical universal taxonomy.
- **No single best cadence:** Kumar et al. report higher success association for incremental versus one-shot delegation, while noting that the agent may merely follow user corrections. Zhou et al. find lower time/preference costs for model-scheduled checkpoints in a simulated task set. Neither establishes a universal interaction cadence; they study different mechanisms, outcomes, systems, and populations.
- **Verification is domain-specific:** coding study participants used code inspection, execution, static analysis, and documentation; data analysis participants needed external evidence and hallucination checks; deep research emphasized traceability and steerability. The synthesis that these are varieties of “feedback signal” is an agent grouping, not a tested shared model.
- **Observed activity versus efficacy:** most selected studies are qualitative or descriptive, and several systems papers report participant preference/usability rather than accuracy or quality outcomes. The one clearer comparative checkpoint result is from a simulated environment; coding associations are observational. “Worked” cannot be generalized beyond the endpoint and study context.
- **Source recency and tooling:** agent systems and interfaces change quickly. The reviewed tool-specific studies describe versions/settings and periods in their papers, but current agent behavior may differ. This subplan maps evidence, not present product capabilities.
- **S1 coverage boundary:** the core sample covers coding agents, interactive deep research, data analysis, a conversational information-seeking case, multi-step agent checkpoints, code suggestions, and writing task-role allocation. Dedicated planning cases remain thin; the writing evidence is an EFL learning study whose full text was not accessible, not an autonomous-agent workflow. No S1 claim says these areas are exhaustively mapped. Image and creative-writing loops were set aside as marginal cases. Training/RLHF feedback was not reviewed, as it is a different object of analysis.

- S1 and S7/S8 are explicitly one source cluster; no claim of independent confirmation is made across them. Other sources have distinct author teams and study samples; no shared dataset was identified in the inspected records.
- Source-reported observations, author interpretations, and this synthesis are labeled separately. Cross-study mechanism groupings (C2/C4) are synthesis, not source terminology unless stated as such.
- A disconfirmation/limits pass checked design, results, and limitation sections for the selected empirical work. Adverse or constraining evidence retained includes: user-agent agreement/sycophancy (S1); participant divergence in view preferences (S2); researcher mediation and manual verification burdens (S3); rewording loops that fail to alter agent anchoring and limits to generalization (S4); simulated rather than live checkpoint evaluation (S5); designed tasks and nonrepresentative sample (S6).
