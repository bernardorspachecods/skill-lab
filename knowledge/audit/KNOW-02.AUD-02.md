# Audit

## Question

What concrete cases show people using AI agents in information-work workflows, and what do their feedback patterns and measured outcomes establish? Which effects are attributable to a loop or interaction policy, which are only associated with access to an agent, and which outcomes remain unknown? This bounded case audit separates direct agent workflows from adjacent conversational-AI and interaction-protocol evidence. It does not estimate a general average effect of “loops.”

## Claims and evidence

### Evidence records

**E1: C1–C5 → S1 — human-agent ad creation in a randomized collaborative workspace and live ad test.** Ju & Aral, *Collaborating with AI Agents: A Field Experiment on Teamwork, Productivity, and Performance*, arXiv:2503.18238v3, 2026-02-05, https://arxiv.org/html/2503.18238v3; authors' manuscript. **Design and provenance:** preregistered randomized experiment; 2,234 U.S. Prolific participants assigned to human-AI or human-human dyads and 40-minute creation sessions; 11,024 ads. A second field experiment served ads and captured about 4.9 million impressions. Pairit synchronized chat and artifact editing; model was GPT-4o-2024-08-06, called every 10 seconds with full workspace/chat/history; AI could message, create/edit text, select or generate images, or wait. Methods §§3.1–3.4, lines 176–215. **Workflow/feedback:** initial task was to create ads for a think tank report; human and agent could see shared artifact and chat, react to edits/messages, and iterate. The source does not prescribe a fixed loop or explicit human approval gate; session stopped at 40 minutes or ad submission. AI actions were semi-autonomous inside the shared workspace, with the person able to communicate/edit/select/submit. **Observed practice:** H-AI teams communicated more overall, used 25% more task-oriented and 18% fewer social/interpersonal messages, delegated 17% more work to AI and made 62% fewer direct text edits; delegation and recognition patterns were observed/mediated, not randomized loop treatments (Abstract; §§4.3–4.4, lines 374–431). **Author-reported outcomes:** H-AI participants produced 50% more ads per worker; text ratings were higher and image ratings lower; output diversity was lower. Field results showed higher text quality predicted click-through/view-through engagement, while better images predicted lower cost per click; overall ad performance did not clearly differ between team conditions (Abstract, lines 99–105; §4.1, lines 296–322; §4.2 and Appendix I). **Comparator/resources:** human-human pairs, same platform, time limit, task, workspace and incentives; not human-alone and not an agent/no-feedback condition. Agent prompts, model/tool use, human time, and all edits were logged, but the trial identifies the effect of AI partner assignment, not the loop or delegation mechanism separately. **Relation:** strong direct evidence that a shared-artifact, iterative agent workflow changes work patterns and task-specific outcomes. **Does not establish:** that iterative feedback caused productivity/quality changes, that more user turns improve outcome, or that findings transfer beyond ad creation/one model. Authors say mediation pathways are post hoc/correlational; recognition vs communication cannot be fully disentangled; lab workflow may not represent long-term collaboration; no human-alone condition (§5.2, lines 474–485). **Evidence type/status:** direct randomized agent-collaboration evidence, mixed endpoint outcomes; it does not isolate feedback policy. Confidence: high for experimental H-AI vs H-H differences in this task; low for attributing effects to loop mechanics.

**E2: C1–C5 → S2 — timing user confirmation in multi-step agents.** Zhou et al., *When Should Users Check? Modeling Confirmation Frequency in Multi-Step Agentic AI Tasks*, CHI 2026, DOI 10.1145/3772318.3790655; arXiv:2510.05307v4 (2026-09-15), https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05307v4. **Design and provenance:** formative study with eight participants across five agent-task cases, then within-subject comparison with 48 participants across shopping, image editing, and Overcooked tasks. Crucially, the confirm-frequency comparison uses a simulated environment with fixed execution time and accuracy to isolate confirmation timing (paper §§3.2, 6.1–6.5, lines 180–205, 355–380). **Workflow/feedback:** agent performs a sequence; user sees action history/nodes and associated screenshots; with the model-selected intermediate policy, user confirms states at checkpoints, or identifies the first wrong step, corrects it, and triggers redo of dependent actions. User keeps control of confirm/correct actions; task ends at completion. The formative observations name Confirmation–Diagnosis–Correction–Redo (CDCR). **Observed use:** all 40 formative trials entered confirmation, 35/40 continued to diagnosis, 33/40 involved correction. Five cases had ambiguous confirmability or missed the actual first error; two Overcooked users skipped steps using domain knowledge; in two document-processing cases users downloaded and manually fixed a minor error rather than request agent redo (§4.2, lines 219–227). **Author-reported outcomes:** modeled intermediate confirmation reduced total completion time by 13.54% (35.84 s) versus end-only confirmation; 81% preferred it. Effects depended on error location: about 29% time reduction for early errors, about 2% for mid-task errors, and a 4.5% time increase for late errors. Checkpointing raised confirmation time but reduced diagnosis time (17%) and redo time (22%) (§7.1–7.2, lines 392–414). **Comparator/resources:** direct schedule comparison with the same simulated agent accuracy, time, task length and error scenarios; checkpoints add user inspection time and may add interruptions, while fewer full-history diagnosis and redo steps offset the cost. It is the closest available direct test of a loop component; it does not compare against no agent, and the simulation holds system quality/time fixed. **Relation:** direct evidence that targeted confirmation can improve time/preference in the study setup, particularly when errors occur early; reported mid-error benefit was near zero and late-error timing was adverse. **Does not establish:** live-world quality/safety improvements, effect in knowledge work, or universal check frequency. Tasks were simulated, one no-error trial was included, task domains and error timing were bounded, and model/user parameter realism is discussed as a limitation (§§6–8). **Evidence type/status:** controlled direct loop-timing comparison; positive overall, heterogeneous/near-null/adverse by error timing. Confidence: moderate for simulated completion-time result; low for transfer.

**E3: C1, C3–C5 → S3 — how experienced developers oversee professional software agents.** Dhanorkar, Passi & Vorvoreanu, *Human Oversight of Agentic Systems in Practice: Examining the Oversight Work, Challenges, and Heuristics of Developers Using Software Agents*, arXiv:2606.05391v1 (2026-06-03), https://arxiv.org/html/2606.05391. **Design/provenance:** semi-structured interviews and inductive coding with 17 experienced software-agent users, 16 using agents weekly or more; 12 participants from authors' large technology employer, five from other firms. 60-minute average interviews, July–August 2025; qualitative, intensity sample (§3, lines 149–164). **Workflow/feedback:** cases included PRD/feature drafting, coding, debugging, log analysis, refactoring and codebase learning (Appendix B, lines 628–637). Participants use instructions/settings and task decomposition before execution; during action, use progress/runtime cues and occasional pause/context correction; after action, inspect diffs, tests, code or artifacts and re-prompt/fix. Human chooses task, configures boundaries, steers, reviews, and can pause or stop. No common agent stop rule was tested. **Observed practice:** the authors' analytic organization identifies a priori control, co-planning, real-time monitoring, and post hoc review (findings §4.1, lines 171–177; Appendix D). Co-planning commonly includes iterative plan refinement and task decomposition (§4.1.2, lines 188–205). Developers rarely performed real-time monitoring; quick scans or omission were common, relying on ad hoc cues like long runs or turns (§4.1.3, lines 213–223). Post hoc review included diff inspection, LLM judges and layered tests; with every fix/re-generation users felt compelled to re-review whole code, increasing effort (§4.1.4, lines 227–239). **Author-reported outcome:** no task quality/productivity comparison was performed. The outcome is reported practice, oversight burden and perceived risk; participants described hidden errors, unreliable reasoning traces, limited control, cognitive distance, overtrust in unfamiliar domains, and workarounds/heuristics (§4.2, lines 265–270; §5, lines 271–279). **Comparator/resources:** no experimental comparator; participants used varied agents/tools in well-resourced professional settings, and monetary/computational cost mattered little. **Relation:** direct real-work evidence about when/how people use loops and the costs/failure modes of review; not efficacy evidence. **Does not establish:** frequencies representative of all developers, performance benefit, or general best practice. Sample is small, expert-heavy, company-skewed, and early in agent adoption; interview recall and organizational context matter (§§3.1, 6, lines 152–158, 307–309). **Evidence type/status:** qualitative observed/reported practice, adverse constraints, no measured loop outcome. Confidence: moderate for these participants' practices; low for prevalence/causal claims.

**E4: C1–C5 → S4 — user intervention patterns in CowPilot-mediated web-task sessions.** Huq et al., *Modeling Distinct Human Interaction in Web Agents*, arXiv:2602.17588v4 (2026-07-07), https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17588. **Design/provenance:** CowCorpus, participant trajectories in CowPilot-mediated web-task sessions: 400 web-navigation tasks with over 4,200 interleaved human and agent actions, collected using CowPilot's suggest-then-execute browser workflow; 20 annotators each attempted 10 fixed and 10 free-form tasks. Users could approve, pause, intervene, or take over at arbitrary points (§3.1, Appendix A.1–A.2, lines 129–162, 314–362). Same dataset supports this trajectory analysis and agent training; count as one provenance cluster. **Workflow/feedback:** tasks included information access, product/service interaction, price search, job search, travel, and drafts/actions on websites. Human observes proposed/action state and intervenes for incorrect/premature actions, stuck/redundant actions, or preference mismatch; user may clarify, correct, take over, or return control to agent (§3.2.2, lines 184–188). Stopping is task completion or continued user takeover; a universal stopping rule is not tested. **Observed use:** analysis identifies hands-off supervision, hands-on oversight, collaborative task-solving, and full takeover. Human actions were 21.63% of steps on standardized tasks and 16.06% on free-form tasks; agent/human time and intervention timing are reported (§3.1–3.3, lines 157–162 and relevant results). This is descriptive, not a randomized comparison of user styles. **Agent change and outcome:** authors train intervention-aware models on CowCorpus and compare against baseline models for intervention prediction (61.4–63.4% improvement over base LMs; §4, abstract/§7). Then they add a learned intervention-aware module to the same execution agent and deploy PlowPilot as a Chrome extension. In n=16 follow-up user sessions, PlowPilot scored on average 36.8% higher across six subjective usefulness/control/alignment dimensions than CowPilot (§5, lines 241–250). The execution agent remained unchanged, so this is a small feature comparison, but outcomes are perceived ratings—not objective completion/quality. **Comparator/resources:** baseline CowPilot vs same executor + intervention-awareness module, same standard/free-form task protocol. The follow-up has only four returning annotators plus 12 new users; before/after ratings and user composition confound pure within-person comparison. More human intervention is not automatically beneficial; the policy tries to predict when to offer the hook. **Relation:** direct case for user feedback as a behavioral signal and intervention timing as a system design variable; preliminary subjective outcome improvement. **Does not establish:** task success, accuracy, time, or cost improvement; no null/adverse outcome inference from a small subjective user study. **Limitations:** 400 tasks are annotator-mediated and task mix is concentrated in web use; 16-person study is preliminary; takeover cluster has sparse intervention data (11/131 steps), limiting model evidence (Appendix A; §4.3, lines 237–249). **Evidence type/status:** direct trace corpus plus small comparative user study; positive perception outcome, objective efficacy unmeasured. Confidence: moderate for the trajectory patterns, low for 36.8% transfer or causal generalization.

**E5: C1, C3–C6 → S5 — developer-agent interaction observed across issue work.** Kumar et al., *Why AI Agents Still Need You: Findings from Developer-Agent Collaborations in the Wild*, arXiv:2506.12347v3 (2025-10-09), accepted ASE 2025, https://arxiv.org/html/2506.12347v3. **Design/provenance:** one-company exploratory study observing 19 professional developers resolving 33 open issues with Cursor v0.47/Claude 3.5 Sonnet; sessions up to 60 minutes; 269 prompts; qualitative coding and descriptive associations (Methods §§III-C–G, lines 193–238). This source also appeared in S1; same participants/source, not independent corroboration. **Workflow/feedback:** participants gave requirements/context, agent searched/edited/ran tools, user followed action trajectory, reviewed code, refined prompts, manually edited, or accepted a patch; study protocol asked participants to continue until satisfied enough to submit where possible. **Observed use:** authors distinguish one-shot delegation from incremental resolution (divide an issue into sequential subtasks/prompts); strategies sometimes combine (§IV-A1, lines 245–260). **Author-reported outcomes:** success rate was 83% for issues handled incrementally vs 38% one-shot; requests for code refinement were associated with 68% success vs 30% without. These are descriptive associations, not inferential/causal effects (§IV-C/Table II, lines 347–362; §III-F, lines 225–229). Participants sometimes used correction to steer agent toward user intent; authors note sycophantic agreement may substitute compliance for substantive problem-solving (§V-C, lines 390–394). Potentially destructive tool actions and environment corruption are documented (§IV-B2, lines 326–332). **Comparator/resources:** within one small observational sample, no randomized assignment, no matched effort/compute/turn budget, and issue difficulty/experience/task strategy could explain results. **Relation:** direct evidence for task decomposition, iteration and review patterns; supports association only. **Does not establish:** incremental prompting itself caused higher success or that it is always preferable. One-company sample, early tool exposure, observer/forced-use effects, task difficulty and tool/model period limit transfer (III-G, lines 232–238). **Evidence type/status:** direct observational/descriptive; positive association with adverse counterevidence; low causal strength. Confidence: moderate for reported observed behaviors; low for strategy effect.

**E6: C1–C4, C6 → S6 — interactive deep research/information seeking.** Pan et al., *InterDeepResearch: Enabling Human-Agent Collaborative Information Seeking through Interactive Deep Research*, arXiv:2603.12608v1 (2026-03-13), preprint, https://arxiv.org/html/2603.12608v1. **Design/provenance:** system paper; formative observations and 15-user study with frequent deep-research users; users selected a task, used the system, completed it, then rated views/interactions and discussed use (§8.1–8.3, lines 278–300). **Workflow/feedback:** user supplies a research goal; agent performs multi-step web searches/research actions; interface exposes action graph/session state and evidence links; user can inspect, clarify/steer during research, or request context backtrace from a generated claim to raw information (§3.2, lines 126–139; §§4.1–4.3, lines 208–225). User ends when task is completed; a criterion for research adequacy is not reported. Agent autonomy is multistep research with user controls. **Observed use:** participants asked for real-time direction changes, pausing and additional requirements; at least one report describes iterative task optimization and following a newly discovered source lead (§8.3 and §9, lines 316–321). **Author-reported outcome:** research-action graph view received 4.7/5 among participants; preferences for process-flow views varied. Evaluation is perception/usability, not objective answer accuracy or task completion quality (§8.3, lines 291–300, 320–324). **Comparator/resources:** no no-feedback or alternative-loop condition; system benchmark performance does not isolate user collaboration. Tool/model/turn/compute differences are not a loop comparison. **Relation:** direct research-workflow evidence for steering, observability and evidence tracing as loop capabilities; not evidence that they improve research outcomes. **Does not establish:** accuracy, time savings, or a universally preferred interaction design. Small trained user sample, preprint, self-selected tasks and descriptive ratings limit inference. **Evidence type/status:** direct user study; perceived usefulness only. Confidence: moderate for documented design and expressed preferences, low for efficacy.

**E7: C1, C3–C6 → S7 — agentic AI escalation and human intervention in customer service.** Wang et al., *Agentic AI and Human-in-the-Loop Interventions: Field Experimental Evidence from Alibaba's Customer Service Operations*, arXiv:2605.14830 (2026-05-14), https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14830; SSRN abstract at https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6745878. **Access/design:** arXiv abstract reports a randomized field experiment on Taobao; the full paper was not accessible in this pass. Workers in treatment supervised an agent resolving AI-eligible chats and handled ineligible chats; controls handled all chats without the agent. **Workflow/feedback:** service agent handles eligible chats and transfers/escalates unresolved or emotional cases to a human; worker's response to the customer is the next corrective action. Exact trigger, timing manipulation, per-case signal, worker caseload, agent tool/compute budget, human stop rule, and outcome measurement definitions cannot be verified from the accessible abstract. **Author-reported outcomes (abstract only):** deployment reduced average chat duration, had limited effects on retrial rates, and substantially lowered ratings for AI-eligible chats. Human intervention preserved quality for technical escalations, but was less effective for emotional/frustration escalations. In emotional escalations workers sent fewer messages, contributed fewer chat rounds, and were less proactive in information-seeking/solution provision; early intervention was essential for sustaining post-escalation effort. A positive spillover to AI-ineligible chats was also reported. **Comparator/resources:** randomized agent deployment vs no-agent workers, but no intervention-policy randomization is stated in abstract. Total system/worker resources and response timing definition are unavailable. **Relation:** direct workplace case demonstrating heterogeneous effects and human re-entry timing/effort as material; not a clean loop-policy effect. **Does not establish:** exact causal contribution of a particular loop because the treatment is bundled AI deployment and intervention behavior; nor transfer outside one platform, service setting, and study. **Evidence type/status:** randomized field experiment, abstract-only extraction; mixed/adverse findings. Confidence: low-to-moderate until full text/methods are inspected.

**E8: C1, C5–C6 → S8 — one knowledge-work task allocation experiment with Copilot (adjacent, not an autonomous agent).** *Scaffolding Human–AI Collaboration: A Field Experiment on Behavioral Protocols and Cognitive Reframing*, arXiv:2604.08678 (2026), https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08678. **Design/provenance:** 388 employees at a Fortune 500 retailer randomized to bundled conditions using Microsoft Copilot; article is an arXiv paper. Task A pairs created an organizational AI-adoption action plan; Task B individuals drafted a strategic communication response. **Interaction tested:** Task A's behavioral “Create-Out-Loud” protocol mandated synchronous discussion then joint AI drafting; control pairs used Copilot naturally. Task B cognitive training reframed AI as thought partner vs basic Copilot training. This is a protocol/interaction scaffold, not an agent executing multi-step tool actions; feedback signal was conversation, shared draft and participant judgment. Stop rule was completion within allotted time; human remained drafter/reviewer. **Outcome:** Task A treatment pairs scored lower on document quality (mean 10.68 vs 15.63; b=-4.96, p<.001) and were much less likely to produce a document (OR=.12); they produced fewer versions/words, while the protocol was difficult to coordinate. For Task B, continuous quality effect was null (p=.223) under strong ceiling (68% score 20/20); a binary perfect-score result was positive but exploratory and threshold-sensitive (§§4.1–4.3, lines 211–285, 337–363). **Comparator/resources:** AI access in both arms, but protocol bundled with AM/PM session (control AM, treatment PM); differential attrition and coordination/time costs confound interpretation. It cannot isolate iteration alone. **Relation:** adjacent adverse/null evidence against assuming a prescribed collaborative feedback protocol benefits knowledge work. **Does not establish:** loop effects in autonomous agents. Authors flag non-preregistration, AM/PM confound, attrition, ceiling and LLM grading limitations. Confidence: moderate that the package performed worse on this task under study conditions; low that the protocol itself alone caused the decrement.

**E9: C1, C4–C6 → S9 — accept, reject, or revise AI suggestions in a programming exam (adjacent conversational assistant, not autonomous agent).** Qian & Wexler, *Take It, Leave It, or Fix It: Measuring Productivity and Trust in Human-AI Collaboration*, IUI 2024, DOI 10.1145/3640543.3645198, Google Research primary PDF: https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-media/pubtools/7713.pdf. **Design/provenance:** task-based mixed-method study with 76 software engineers at one large tech company, Java readability exam, 10 multiple-choice questions, within-person two-pass Bard-vs-book access; conditions/question order randomized (Methods §§3.1–3.4, PDF pp. 1–2, lines 141–221). Bard does not take tools/actions, so this is adjacent. **Workflow/feedback:** users get a Bard answer or documentation/search answer, can revise after a second pass and view correct answer/explanation after each question; resource access/time and correctness are feedback. User chooses whether to accept/change/skip; no autonomous-agent loop. **Observed practice/outcome:** adding Bard had no significant effect for straightforward “search” items; documentation access improved them, especially experts. Bard improved “solve”-type performance for novices but had no effect for experts. Users spent significantly more time with Bard, despite reporting greater perceived productivity/efficiency; increased reliance across exam, automation complacency, and experts' rejection behavior are reported (Results §§4.1–4.3, PDF pp. 3–5, lines 243–349; abstract, pp. 0–1). Some users changed answers in the second pass; verification resource quality and expertise mattered. **Comparator/resources:** randomized access to Bard vs conventional “Book” resources on second pass; task, time limit and participant fixed, but each condition changes information source, not feedback-loop policy. Bard had latency and verbose interaction costs; measures include actual correctness and time vs subjective experience. **Relation:** useful adjacent evidence that more conversational rounds do not automatically yield better correctness and that the external verification signal/expertise changes what users should accept. **Does not establish:** autonomous-agent practice or an iterative-agent effect. Sample is one company, one exam/technology, short session; the exam forced multiple-choice and two passes. Confidence: moderate for reported condition effects in this exam, low for transfer to agents.

**E10: C1, C3–C6 → S10 — researcher-mediated data analysis with a conversational assistant (adjacent, not tool-using agent).** Drosos et al., *“It’s like a rubber duck that talks back”: Understanding Generative AI-Assisted Data Analysis Workflows through a Participatory Prompting Study*, CHIWORK 2024, DOI 10.1145/3663384.3663389; primary PDF: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/drosos_2024_bing_participatory_prompting.pdf. **Design/provenance:** N=15 participants used Bing Chat on self-selected spreadsheet/data-sensemaking tasks in researcher-mediated participatory prompting sessions; participants reflected aloud each turn (§3.1, §3.4, PDF pp. 3–5; §6, pp. 18–19). **Workflow/feedback:** participant states data/goal; researcher formulates/types the prompt; AI answers or proposes search/analysis; participant evaluates, requests changes, and pivots between information foraging and hypothesis/test loops. No autonomous tool execution is observed; the study protocol's user-specific stopping criterion is not reported. **Author-reported findings:** GenAI reduced manual effort for information gathering and helped ideate hypotheses/test strategies; participants had barriers in prompt/context formulation, response relevance, and verifying claims/hallucination (§4.2 and §6, PDF pp. 8, 18–19, lines 731–753, 1617–1636). No randomized comparator or objective causal outcome. **Resources/comparator:** researcher support and prompting intervention are extra human resources; no unmediated control. **Relation:** adjacent evidence maps signal differences and shows the potentially positive role of query/hypothesis iteration alongside verification cost. **Does not establish:** agent-loop efficacy or real-world independent use; researcher mediation lowers external validity (limitations §6, PDF pp. 17–18, lines 1588–1616). Confidence: moderate for mediated workflow observations, low for generalization.

**E11: C1, C4–C6 → S11 — single-analyst questionnaire analysis with ChatGPT 5.4 (direct human–LLM case, adjacent to autonomous tool-using agents).** Tomasi, *AI-in-the-human-loop: an operational model for mature human-AI collaboration*, *Journal of Ambient Intelligence and Humanized Computing* (2026), https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12652-026-05135-x. **Design/provenance:** retrospective case using 11 documented chats, 30 human turns and 30 LLM turns from one real questionnaire-analysis project. The operational team was a single analyst and ChatGPT 5.4, described by the paper as a conversational operational assistant; two additional LLMs later acted as judges on the logs (§4.1–4.3, lines 173–214). **Workflow/feedback:** analyst defined goals, chose artifacts, evaluated answers, reused/corrected/reformulated; the model supported initial method orientation, question-specific analysis, summaries, subgroup ideas, charts/scripts. Work moved from broad framing to data audit, item-type analysis, and synthesis; stop conditions/work hours/resources not standardized. The case does not establish autonomous tool execution. **Reported observations:** interaction evolved toward more specific requests and deliverables; the two judge models identified contextual gaps and points where proactive clarification would help. The paper explicitly says two judges are not independent operational agents and agreement is not a benchmark; human researchers checked outputs against transcripts (§4.3–4.4, lines 205–238). **Comparator/resources:** no control, no objective outcome-quality/time comparison, no resource budget; retrospective LLM-as-judge risks are acknowledged. **Relation:** a direct user–LLM iteration example in a real knowledge task, but only adjacent to autonomous agent workflows. **Does not establish:** prevalence or efficacy; single analyst, one task/model, 11 chats, retrospective analysis, and judge bias limit generality (§5.2, lines 238–239). Confidence: low for effectiveness; moderate as an existence case.

**E12: C1, C3–C6 → S12 — general GPT-4 access in consulting tasks (adjacent; no tool-using agent or loop manipulation).** Dell'Acqua et al., *Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier: Field Experimental Evidence of the Effects of Artificial Intelligence on Knowledge Worker Productivity and Quality*, *Organization Science*, published online 2026-03-11, DOI 10.1287/orsc.2025.21838, https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2025.21838. **Design/provenance:** preregistered randomized lab-in-the-field experiment with 758 knowledge workers doing realistic consulting tasks; conditions no AI, GPT-4, or GPT-4 plus prompt-engineering overview (abstract, lines 338–355, 375–393). The paper is not an agent-loop study and is retained only as task-boundary contrast. **Outcome:** within-capability tasks showed increased completion/quality/speed; for one beyond-capability managerial task, participants with AI were 19 percentage points less likely to produce correct answers (abstract, lines 339–344; results lines 628–633, 866–875). **Comparator/resources:** AI access and prompt-engineering overview are manipulated, no loop policy; authors report capability-matched task effects. **Relation:** supports the need to separate task capability and verification from claims that more interaction itself prevents harm. **Does not establish:** any feedback-loop effect or use of autonomous agents. One firm, one outside-frontier task, GPT-4 June 2023, and task-specific scoring limit transfer. Confidence: high for study's measured task effects, none for loop efficacy.

### Academic finding cards (F# links to canonical evidence)

**F1 — Checkpoints can reduce rework when mistakes surface early; value falls or reverses later.** The only direct controlled loop-policy comparison in this set found 13.54% lower average simulated completion time with model-scheduled intermediate confirmation than end-only confirmation, but the advantage was concentrated in early errors; the mid-error change was minimal and late errors cost 4.5% more time. This is bounded evidence for timing/cost tradeoffs, not a universal checkpoint recommendation. Evidence: E2.

**F2 — Users show varied intervention styles, and agent intervention modeling may improve perceived usefulness.** CowCorpus documents hands-off supervision through full takeover, including correction of agent errors, preference clarification, and breaking stuck/redundant actions. A model predicting intervention points added to the same execution agent was rated more useful by 16 users, but the endpoint was subjective and the follow-up was small. Evidence: E4.

**F3 — Real developers combine pre-action planning, sparse monitoring, and post hoc review; review work can grow with each repair.** Interviewed developers decomposed tasks and iteratively clarified plans, but rarely monitored reasoning in real time; they often scanned quickly and then reviewed outputs/diffs/tests. Re-generating a fix could require reviewing the whole change again. These are reported practices/costs, not demonstrated performance effects. Evidence: E3.

**F4 — Incremental prompting is associated with success in one coding-agent study, but causal attribution is unavailable.** Incremental issue resolution and requested refinements co-occurred with higher descriptive success rates in a small observational study. Issue selection, expertise, number of turns, human edits and agent behavior are potential alternative explanations. Evidence: E5.

**F5 — Agent collaboration can raise throughput and some quality outcomes while worsening other quality dimensions.** In randomized ad creation, AI-human teams were more productive per human and had better-rated text, but worse image ratings and lower output diversity. The agent workflow included more task-oriented talk and delegation, but the study did not randomize feedback policy. Evidence: E1.

**F6 — Direct research and analysis cases currently support workflow affordances and user-reported needs more than efficacy.** Deep-research participants wanted visibility and steering; data-analysis participants found assistance for foraging/hypothesis work but also had verification and context-formulation burdens. Neither establishes a causal loop benefit on accuracy, time, or quality. Evidence: E6, E10.

**F7 — Mandatory collaboration protocols can impose costs, and assistant evidence should not be silently generalized to agents.** A workplace Copilot protocol experiment found lower pair-document scores and production under a tightly structured synchronous protocol, while its individual task's primary continuous outcome was null under ceiling and attrition limitations. This is adjacent evidence, not a tool-using agent loop result. Evidence: E8.

### Outcome categories located

- **Positive direct results:** intermediate checkpoints reduced simulated time overall and were preferred (E2); intervention-aware timing raised perceived web-agent usefulness in a small study (E4); randomized AI teammate increased ad output per worker/text scores (E1); Taobao field experiment reports shorter chats and preservation of service quality for technical escalations (E7). Each is a distinct endpoint and design.
- **Mixed / conditional direct results:** Pairit text gains coexisted with lower image quality and less diversity (E1); confirmation schedule gains depended on error timing and task domain (E2); intervention styles and intervention value differ by user/task (E4); technical vs emotional escalation had different outcomes (E7); user research preferences varied (E6).
- **Adverse direct results:** late-error checkpoint policy increased completion time by 4.5% in the simulated subset (E2); service ratings for AI-eligible conversations fell overall and human response was less effective for emotional escalation (E7); interviews report automation bias, missed errors, cognitive distance and repeated review burden (E3); coding-agent corrections may elicit sycophantic agreement, and terminal actions can disrupt work (E5).
- **Null direct outcome evidence:** no retrieved direct-agent study here cleanly reports a powered null effect for the causal contribution of a feedback loop to information-work task quality. Pairit's field ad outcomes showed no clear overall performance difference between human-AI and human-human ads, despite offsetting text/image quality patterns (E1); that is a null-like result for an agent-collaboration outcome, not for a feedback policy. Zhou's mid-error time benefit was minimal (~2%), but the study's overall intervention remained beneficial; it is not evidence of a broad null. Several direct studies do not measure the relevant outcome at all; unmeasured is not null.
- **Adjacent null/mixed/adverse evidence:** Bard had no significant effect for search-type questions and took longer despite perceived speed (E9); Copilot's individual Task B continuous quality outcome was null under ceiling/attrition/rater limits, and a structured pair protocol performed worse on document quality/production (E8); GPT-4 reduced correctness by 19 percentage points on one outside-capability task (E12). These are not direct agent-loop effect estimates.

## Conflicts and limitations

- **Loop-specific vs access-to-AI effect:** Zhou et al. directly compare confirmation schedules. Pairit, Kumar, Alibaba, and BCG compare agent/AI access or collaboration types; CowCorpus compares an intervention-aware module but only subjective outcomes; interview and system studies document practice or perceptions. The latter cannot support a causal “feedback loop works” claim by themselves.
- **More feedback is not always better:** checkpointing trades check time against diagnostic/redo time; early failures create more recoverable value than late ones. Pairit shows more task-oriented collaboration/delegation can improve some outputs while reducing variation and image quality. Dhanorkar interviews report frequent re-review costs; oversights and quick scans can miss defects.
- **Feedback-signal quality matters:** source systems provide differing signals—tests/diffs, visible action state, user preference, source trace, service-customer feedback, or model output. A repeated prompt is not equivalent to external correctness evidence. Adjacent Bard data shows authoritative documentation can outperform a conversational response on fact-search questions.
- **Heterogeneity:** task type, agent capability, error timing, user expertise/style, escalation type, and modality alter effects. The evidence is too diverse to pool effect sizes across time, quality ratings, satisfaction, accuracy, and production counts.
- **Provenance:** Pairit has a randomized workspace trial and a downstream field ad test, but both are one study/provenance cluster. CowCorpus trajectories, intervention model, and PlowPilot user study are one dataset/system line. Kumar appears in S1 but is one study/sample, not independent evidence. E3 interviews, E1 Pairit, and E2 checkpoint work are distinct teams/samples. Several adjacent records are used only for boundary conditions.
- **Access and currentness:** Alibaba S7 was abstract-only; no exact mechanism/outcome definitions can be independently checked from its full text here. Springer case E11 was read as an exploratory single-case study, not a deployment benchmark. Model behavior changes over time; evidence records state model/system versions where reported.
- **Search coverage:** the working log retains exact queries/routes. Coverage spans several direct task contexts and includes a refutation pass. It is not systematic review, no recall estimate, and citation chaining is selective. Direct causal evidence in research, planning, writing, and analysis remains thin; no direct null-effect study of loop contribution was located.

## Validation

- Every material `C#` links to one or more `E#` entries. Each `E#` names source/version/location, design, directness, signal/action/stop/control, outcomes, comparator/resources, what it does/does not establish, and evidence limits.
- Author-reported results are separated from analytic relation/transfer statements. Descriptive associations (Kumar; Pairit mediations) are not stated as causal loop effects. The simulated checkpoint policy is distinguished from live deployments. User ratings are distinguished from objective task performance.
- The outcome inventory names positive, mixed, adverse, and null/gap evidence; it does not classify unmeasured outcomes as null. Adjacent cases are visibly marked and excluded from direct loop claims.
- No direct study in this collection independently establishes a universally effective loop or loop benefit across information tasks. The strongest loop-specific causal evidence is checkpoint timing in a simulated multi-step task set; the strongest broader real-work experimental evidence randomizes AI collaboration but does not isolate feedback policy.
- **Stop rationale:** Collection followed broad agent-human workflow searches, task-family routes (software, web, research/search, analysis, service, creative production), direct loop/checkpoint comparison routes, and adverse/null/adjacent contrast routes. It found direct qualitative, observational, randomized workflow, feature-comparison, and small user-study evidence. Further browsing would add cases but is less likely to change the current evidence distinction: one direct checkpoint policy experiment, several agent-use outcome studies where loop behavior is measured but not manipulated, and several direct reports of practice without outcome comparison. The principal gap is not addressed by asserting more cases: causal loop comparisons in research, planning, writing, and analysis; full-text extraction for S7; and longer-term resource/accounting evidence remain outstanding. This is an adequacy judgment for S2, not a claim of complete literature coverage.
