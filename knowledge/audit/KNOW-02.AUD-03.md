# Audit

## Question

Under what conditions do feedback and revision loops in information work help, fail, or remain unproven? This cross-case audit emphasizes signal quality, independent verification, iteration/stopping, human effort/control, proxy alignment, and failure recovery. S1 and S2 are starting evidence records, not a closed search set. The analysis distinguishes direct user-facing workflow evidence from transfer evidence on human–AI judgment and model-internal refinement.

### Findings

**C1 — Signal quality changes the direction of effects (moderate confidence, bounded adjacent evidence).** A repeated human–AI interaction experiment with emotion judgments found that accurate AI feedback improved participants' later judgments against independently defined motion evidence, while biased feedback increased their bias; noisy and biased conditions did not differ reliably on some of the study's bias/accuracy measures. Participants answered before seeing the model output, reducing the chance that the result was merely copying. A Stable Diffusion exposure experiment also shifted participant judgments toward an overrepresented group, but that outcome has no definitive ground truth and is better described as a shift toward a potentially biased representation than as objectively measured error. This offers a mechanism-level warning and a conditional positive case, not evidence that repeated feedback improves an agent user's deliverable. [E1, E2]

**C2 — Specific feedback plus guardrails and user choice has one positive live-workflow result (moderate confidence for ICLR review editing; low transfer confidence).** In the ICLR 2025 randomized deployment, Review Feedback Agent supplied optional comments about vague critiques, possible misreadings, and professionalism. Feedback had to pass its reliability checks; if it failed, the pipeline retried once and withheld the comment after a second failure. Reviewers retained the final decision. About 26.6% of reviewers who successfully received feedback made a substantive update; an LLM-based incorporation analysis estimated that 89% of those updaters incorporated at least one suggestion. In a selected blind-comparison subset, two human AI researchers preferred the revised review 89% of 100 paired examples, describing it as clearer/more informative. This blind review is independent of the agent's own reliability tests, but it is a subjective quality judgment on a selected subset, not factual correctness or downstream paper quality. In the overall intent-to-treat comparison, feedback selection raised update rate and review length; average score changes did not differ significantly. Fewer feedback items were associated with higher incorporation. Thus targeted, optional and bounded feedback can help a particular revision task, while the many non-updaters, selection of edited reviews for blind evaluation, and lack of an independent correctness endpoint limit the broader claim. [E4]

**C3 — Iteration is conditional on the signal and verification (moderate confidence for benchmarks; low direct-workflow transfer).** In 2023 reasoning benchmarks, Huang et al. found that intrinsic self-correction without oracle labels reduced accuracy for GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4-Turbo, and Llama-2 in the tested settings. One extra round already reduced GPT-3.5 GSM8K accuracy from 75.9% to 75.1%; two rounds reduced it to 74.7%, and accuracy also fell on CommonSenseQA and HotpotQA. By contrast, oracle-labeled checks improved scores, but those use ground truth unavailable in ordinary work. In an adjacent 2026 code-review benchmark with paired canonical and buggy solutions, asking models to explain and repair increased false rejection of correct code in some model/task combinations; GPT-4o's false-negative rate rose from 26.2% to 73.2% on HumanEval and 35.9% to 87.9% on MBPP when rationale/repair was required, while false acceptance fell. A separate bounded Detect–Repair–Verify study compared single-pass and iterative LLM repairs under matched budgets using executable function and exploit tests. Iteration increased secure-and-correct outcomes in some conditions, especially for narrower file/requirement scopes, but project-level repair was unstable and some cases failed to converge within four rounds or introduced functional regressions. These are model/agent benchmark workflows, not human-agent collaboration trials; taken together they show that a retry is useful when it can act on actionable new evidence and every update can be checked, rather than by iteration alone. [E5, E6, E8]

**C4 — Human involvement is not equivalent to effective verification (moderate confidence in stylized decisions; transfer limited).** In a randomized forecasting experiment, 292 participants could either delegate to human/algorithm recommendations or edit a recommendation before submitting. Adding the human-edit option increased preference for algorithmic recommendations by 7 percentage points and reported confidence, yet final accuracy was worse in the human-in-loop condition (mean absolute error 18.0 vs 17.4; p=.04). Participants intervened less on the largest errors and made smaller corrections when they did intervene. There was no within-experiment performance feedback, which matters: participants lacked a fresh external signal for deciding which output to correct. This is a stylized decision task and the participants were not agent users; it cautions against treating a person-in-the-loop checkbox as an outcome measure. S2's checkpoint study provides a closer but simulated agent-task boundary: checkpoints saved the most time when an error appeared early, little time for mid-task errors, and increased time for late errors; the study's simulated error and time parameters constrain transfer. [E3; KNOW-02.AUD-02 E2]

**C5 — Measure user value separately from the loop's own proxy (moderate confidence as a design requirement, not a universal empirical effect).** The ICLR trial's word count and agent-generated feedback-incorporation score are process measures; blind human ratings provide a separate, though subjective, quality endpoint. Its mean reviewer-score change was not significantly different between feedback and control, so length or suggestion uptake should not be recast as improved paper decisions. METR's early-2025 randomized coding-assistant trial found tasks took 19% longer (95% CI reported in its 2026 update as +2% to +39%) even as participants expected/estimated time savings; its later study estimated faster completion but authors identified serious selection and time-accounting problems, including multi-agent concurrent work and task selection, and called the estimate a likely bad proxy for productivity. The later estimates were −18% for prior participants (CI −38% to +9%) and −4% for recruits (CI −15% to +9%), and authors say these data are only weak evidence of the speedup's size. These access-to-AI studies do not isolate feedback loops, but directly demonstrate why perceived speed, completion time, quality, rework, and task choice must be kept distinct. S2 also reports objective output measures and subjective ratings separately (Pairit; CowPilot); those trials answer different questions and are not independent replications of one loop effect. [E4, E7; KNOW-02.AUD-02 E1, E2, E4, E5]

**C6 — Stopping evidence is narrow and heterogeneous (low confidence for cross-workflow claims).** The ICLR feedback service documents a concrete bounded policy: at most two full generation attempts, then return an error and withhold feedback if reliability tests still fail; it is a design choice in one deployed system, not a comparative test of optimal stopping. Intrinsic self-correction studies stop after a fixed one or two rounds and show accuracy declines without oracle feedback; code-repair benchmarks use executable tests and matched iteration caps, but effectiveness varies by scope and some cases remain unresolved. S2's checkpoint simulation shows how the value of an intervention can decline as errors arrive later, because remaining work available to redo is smaller. Together these suggest that continuation should depend on new, discriminative evidence and remaining recoverable work, but that is a cross-case inference, not a tested stopping rule for agent users. In the searched direct user-agent workflow evidence, no located trial randomized adaptive stopping versus fixed or user-controlled stopping while measuring both independent task quality and user/resource cost. [E3–E6, E8; KNOW-02.AUD-02 E2]

### Evidence records

**E1: C1 → S1 — condition-dependent human–AI feedback in perceptual judgment (adjacent transfer evidence).** Ahuja et al., “How human–AI feedback loops alter human perceptual, emotional and social judgements,” *Nature Human Behaviour* 9 (2025; online 2024), DOI [10.1038/s41562-024-02077-2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-02077-2); primary article, accessed 2026-10-07. Experiment 2, Results, lines 197–207: participants' bias increased with the biased algorithm relative to baseline and accurate algorithm; interaction with accurate algorithm improved independent motion judgments relative to baseline (mean errors 13.48 vs 15.03, p<.001); participants gave judgments before viewing the AI response. A five-block follow-up found a positive linear trend in bias under biased feedback and accuracy under accurate feedback (lines 199, 204). Methods lines 269–272 give n=120 for Experiment 2, n=50 per accurate/biased condition; follow-up samples described at the result lines. Discussion lines 245–255 explicitly bounds effect: when AI was accurate, accuracy improved; bias amplification requires bias in the system. **Relation:** direct support for signal-quality condition; the outcome is independently scored against correct motion information, not the algorithm's own signal. **Limits:** perceptual task in controlled interaction, not work artifact creation, user-facing agent planning, or resource accounting; no validated generalization to professional tasks. **Status:** supports the bounded condition in C1, not broad practice advice. Confidence: moderate for the experimental mechanism; low for workflow transfer.

**E2: C1 → same S1 provenance cluster — exposure to a real-world image generator (adjacent, weaker outcome criterion).** Ahuja et al., same article and dataset provenance as E1, Experiment 3, lines 220–244: n=100 participants classified likely financial managers before and after viewing generated images; exposure to Stable Diffusion images shifted choices toward White men relative to neutral fractal control (between-group change p=.02, d=.46). Lines 233 and 241–244 state there is no definitive ground truth and report the neutral-image control. **Relation:** shows that a model's representational pattern may shift subsequent user judgments. **Limits:** not an iterative task workflow; the normative error interpretation is not ground-truthed, so do not call it demonstrated accuracy loss. This is not independent corroboration of E1: same authors/research program, paper, and source. Confidence: low-to-moderate as transfer evidence.

**E3: C4, C6 → S2 — delegation versus human editing of algorithmic forecast (adjacent controlled experiment).** Sele & Chugunova, “Putting a human in the loop: Increasing uptake, but decreasing accuracy of automated decision-making,” *PLOS ONE* 19(2), e0298037 (2024), DOI [10.1371/journal.pone.0298037](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0298037); primary open-access article, accessed 2026-10-07. Methods lines 153–190: online U.S. high-school math percentile prediction, public dataset, between-subject delegation vs editable recommendation, 292 total, no accuracy feedback during task. Results lines 201–229: human-in-loop option increased algorithm preference 7 pp; reported confidence increased; final prediction absolute error was 18.0 vs 17.4 in delegation, p=.04. Lines 231–252 report prefilled recommendation, 63% adjusted, and lower chance/smaller correction for larger errors; Discussion lines 284–293 says expertise and information available could change this result. **Relation:** direct support for the possibility that intervention increases uptake/confidence while lowering independent accuracy. Ground-truth student score is independent of recommendation. **Limits:** participants were not professional agents/users; single stylized forecast task; recommendation sources curated to equal average accuracy; no feedback provided; no multi-turn work loop. Confidence: moderate in this task; low for agent workflow transfer.

**E4: C2, C5, C6 → S3 — live AI feedback on ICLR peer-review writing (direct user-facing revision workflow).** Thakkar et al., “Can LLM feedback enhance review quality? A randomized study of 20K reviews at ICLR 2025,” arXiv:2504.09737v1 (2025; expanded/version of record published as *Nature Machine Intelligence* 8, 326–336, 2026), [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09737); primary RCT report inspected 2026-10-07. One study/provenance cluster: the Nature article and arXiv preprint report the same ICLR 2025 deployment and are not separate replications. Methods §2.1 lines 60–81: randomization by submission to no/half/all reviews receiving optional feedback; reviewers could edit before deadline. Agent and reliability system, §2.2, lines 120–144: actor/aggregator/critic/formatter pipeline; max two attempts; only post if all four reliability checks pass; over 96% of generated feedback passed; after a second fail, no comment was posted. Results §3.1, lines 154–162: 22,467 reviews selected for feedback vs 22,364 control; 18,946 received it; 26.6% updated, versus 9.4% control; intent-to-treat definition includes selected reviews without actual feedback. §3.2, lines 173–180: incorporation measured by Claude Sonnet 3.5 classifier, with 89% of updated reviewers incorporating at least one item; two human researchers blindly preferred revised reviews in 89/100 selected pairs (reviews with 3–4 feedback items and >0.60 incorporation). §3.3, lines 168–169 and 183–186: average score changes not significantly different; more reviewer-score edits and longer rebuttal texts. Discussion lines 228–230: feedback optional, reviewer retained control, feedback limited to three categories. **Relation:** direct support that selected, optional, actionable feedback with automated guardrails can lead to better-reviewed revisions in a specific writing process; the blind evaluator is external to the agent's own signal. **Limits:** only 1 conference, feedback categories are narrow; blind comparisons condition on uptake/incorporation and are not the full randomized effect; incorporated suggestions are classified by another LLM; no outcome on factual correctness or paper quality; longer text is not inherently better; score outcomes did not show a significant average change. Confidence: moderate for revision quality in context, low-to-moderate for broader work transfer.

**E5: C3, C6 → S4 — intrinsic self-correction benchmark (adjacent, model-internal loop).** Huang et al., “Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet,” ICLR 2024, arXiv:2310.01798; [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.01798), accessed 2026-10-07. §3.1 lines 73–88: benchmark setup (GSM8K, CommonSenseQA, HotpotQA; GPT-3.5/4/4-Turbo and Llama-2; up to two self-correction rounds). §3.2–3.3, tables 3–6 / lines 96–124: without oracle labels, accuracy declined for all tested models/benchmarks in reported settings; e.g. GPT-3.5 GSM8K 75.9→75.1→74.7; Llama-2 GSM8K 62→43.5→36.5. Correct answers were often changed to wrong answers (lines 113–115). Oracle labels improved performance, but authors note the setting presupposes unavailable ground truth (lines 106–111). **Relation:** model answer accuracy against benchmark truth is independent of self-critique; no external feedback yields adverse results in this benchmark. **Limits:** 2023 model versions, closed-book reasoning benchmarks, prompt conditions, fixed max rounds; does not test a human user, tool-using agent, or work artifact. Results are not a blanket statement that all revision loops fail. Confidence: moderate for the tested benchmark comparisons, low for direct-workflow transfer.

**E6: C3, C6 → S5 — LLM code conformance and repair-prompt over-correction (adjacent model loop).** Fang et al., “Are LLMs reliable code reviewers? systematic overcorrection in requirement conformance judgement,” *Automated Software Engineering* (2026), DOI [10.1007/s10515-026-00638-5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10515-026-00638-5); primary publisher article, accessed 2026-10-07. §3.3–3.4 lines 133–173: >700 tasks, paired canonical/buggy implementations, five models, three prompts from verdict only through explanation and repair; same requirement/code input across prompt modes. §5.1, Table 2 and lines 214–219: FNR is false rejection of correct code and FPR false acceptance of buggy code; GPT-4o FNR increased 26.2→73.2% HumanEval and 35.9→87.9% MBPP with explanation/repair, while FPR dropped nearly zero in some conditions. §5.2 and §8 lines 357–377: executable-test filter reduced over-correction but could increase false acceptance; weak benchmark tests, prompt semantics, and prompt wording affect conclusions. **Relation:** ground-truth labels from paired implementations make the correctness endpoint independent of the model's verdict; illustrates a possible overcorrection mechanism when the task mandates a fix. **Limits:** no human or autonomous agent workflow, no user effort measure, canonical solutions/tests can be incomplete, authors do not isolate actual multi-turn user revision. This single paper is not evidence that all richer prompts worsen quality. Confidence: moderate for benchmark/prompt comparison; low for transfer.

**E7: C5 → S6 — METR task-level randomized coding-assistant studies and 2026 measurement update (same research program; early and later cohorts distinct but not independent corroboration).** Becker et al., “Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity,” [paper/arXiv:2507.09089](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089) and METR [2025 study page](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/), plus Becker et al., [2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/); primary study reports and authors' measurement update inspected 2026-10-07. Original RCT abstract: 16 experienced OSS developers, 246 randomized tasks, task time increased 19% with early-2025 tools, despite participant estimates of 20% savings; authors say robustness analyses make experimental artifact an unlikely primary explanation but cannot rule out artifacts. 2026 update lines 35–44: original 19% longer, CI +2% to +39%; subsequent late-2025 estimate −18% (prior developers, CI −38% to +9%) and −4% (new recruits, CI −15% to +9%), but selection led authors to describe the newer data as weak evidence for the magnitude. Lines 48–64 and 82–84: later sample 57 developers, 143 repositories, 800+ tasks; selective task participation, lower pay, incompatible time accounting when agent waits overlap with other work, quality/task differences; authors call it a likely bad proxy for productivity. **Relation:** task time is independent of model's internal score, but may not capture value/quality; participants' perceived speed diverges from observed time in the early study. **Limits:** AI availability comparison, not feedback-loop manipulation; early and late tools differ; later RCT suffers non-compliance/selection/time-measurement threats; no claim here that loop use caused the changes. Confidence: moderate for early RCT estimate in its setting, low for generalization/latest effect size.

**E8: C3, C6 → S9 — bounded, test-grounded LLM Detect–Repair–Verify workflow (adjacent autonomous artifact repair).** Cheng, “Detect–Repair–Verify for LLM-Generated Code: A Multi-Language, Multi-Granularity Empirical Study,” arXiv:2603.23633v1 (2026-03-24), [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.23633), accessed 2026-10-07. §4.2.2–4.2.4 lines 432–451: loop takes a vulnerability target and prior report/patch, emits a repair, reruns functional and exploit tests, continues only if either check fails, and stops at cap K; success requires both tests. Five language/model benchmark contexts; single-pass and iterative settings use a matched budget. §5.1, Table 10, lines 455–513: iteration improved some failures (e.g., Python project reached secure-and-correct at iteration 3 after single-pass failure; PHP project did not converge within 4; narrower requirement cases included both single-pass and delayed successes/failures). §5.3.2 Tables 17–19 / lines 689–715 and Discussion §6.1–6.3 lines 720–743: scope changes reliability; incomplete repair and regression are observed failure patterns; authors state bounded iteration is useful when the change is verifiable and localized; tests may be incomplete, fixed budgets may leave recoverable cases, and model output varies. **Relation:** executable functional and exploit tests are external to the model's self-report, but the same tests supply feedback to subsequent repair rounds and determine the final pass outcome. Therefore, the final secure-and-correct score is not independent of the loop feedback signal. They still provide an external check against the tests' specified behavior/security criteria, but test coverage can miss defects. The matched budget improves resource comparability, although total compute/human effort is not monetized or measured. **Limits:** benchmark of generated web applications/code artifacts, not a human user steering an agent, no field use, one author, arXiv preprint, model/version-specific and narrow vulnerability/test coverage. A related earlier preprint arXiv:2603.00897 was discovered from the same author/project/repository and appears to share the benchmark/provenance; it is not counted as independent corroboration or separately used. Confidence: moderate for the reported benchmark conditions; low for transfer to user-workflow efficacy.

## Conflicts and limitations

- **Positive, mixed, adverse and null evidence:** Positive evidence is present in E1's accurate-feedback condition, E4's review-editing endpoint, and E8's bounded repair gains in some narrower scopes; E3 and S2 E2 show adverse/conditional human intervention or timing effects; E5 and E6 show adverse model-only refinement/prompt effects. Mixed/null evidence includes no detected baseline/accuracy distinction for some noisy-versus-biased comparisons in E1; no significant average score change in E4; E8's project-level nonconvergence and scope-dependent outcomes; and METR's later confidence intervals that include no effect, under stated selection and measurement limitations. These are not all the same kind of “loop” or outcome.
- **No located category:** No direct user-facing agent-workflow trial located in the searched routes isolated a null effect of an adaptive stopping policy against another stop policy while measuring both independent work quality and user/resource costs. This is a gap in this bounded search, not evidence that such a study does not exist. The plan's located-null requirement is met through null components within relevant studies, while this precise stopping-policy comparison remains unlocated.
- **Direct vs adjacent:** E4 is the strongest direct user-facing feedback/revision evidence found in this S3 search. S1/S2 direct sources include real agent/collaboration studies but often compare access, interface, or intervention features rather than cleanly randomizing a feedback-loop mechanism. E1/E2 are human–AI judgment/exposure tasks; E3 is algorithmic decision support; E5/E6 are model-internal loops. They inform mechanisms and failure modes, not direct efficacy of a user's agent workflow.
- **Feedback quality vs evaluator quality:** E4's reliability checks are LLM-based, so they do not by themselves establish factual reliability. Its blind human pair assessment is independent of that internal gate but assesses perceived clarity/informativeness on a selected set. E6's executable test filter is stronger external evidence than textual critique, but test coverage limits it. No single metric is a universal user-value oracle.
- **Outcome independence:** E1's motion judgment accuracy, E3's true test percentile, E5's benchmark answer labels, and E6's paired code labels are external to the evaluated model's own report/answer. E8's executable functional/exploit tests are external to the model's self-report, but the same results guide subsequent repair rounds and define the final pass score; that score is not independent of the loop feedback signal. Test coverage can miss defects. E2 has a shift outcome but no definitive normative ground truth. E4's word count and incorporation are process measures; its blind human review is separate but subjective. E7 task time is externally measured, but productivity can be misrepresented by omitted quality, task mix, and concurrent waiting.
- **Resource comparability:** E5 adds model calls; E4 includes human reading/editing and rebuttal discussion; E3 requires user adjustment; E6 adds testing; E8 uses matched generation/repair budgets but does not quantify runtime or human effort. None provides a shared accounting of latency, money, human attention, rework, and output quality. Comparing across these studies as if “cost” were one quantity would be invalid.
- **Conflicting results are not direct contradictions:** Accurate vs biased signal can produce opposite changes (E1); a well-gated, optional reviewer suggestion helps a selected writing task (E4), while unsupported intrinsic correction harms benchmark reasoning (E5). These differ in feedback source, signal reliability, task structure, outcome, and user/agent role. METR 2025 and 2026 estimates use different tools/cohorts and the later sample has serious selection; they should be read as changing evidence and measurement instability, not a clean replication contradiction.
- **Evidence not located or not pursued:** Searches did not yield a representative set of production agent traces with independently audited failures and quantified user effort; nor a direct user-facing agent study that factorially varies feedback reliability, initial answer quality, stopping condition, external verification, and recovery. The study scope remains broad and open-web recall was not measured. Searches did not pursue proprietary telemetry, vendor marketing, or broad policy literature where the work-process mechanism was not empirically inspectable.

- Checked claim-to-evidence links: C1 E1–E2; C2 E4; C3 E5–E6/E8; C4 E3 plus prior S2 E2/E3; C5 E4/E7 plus prior S2 E1/E2/E4/E5; C6 E3–E6/E8 plus prior S2 E2. Each record states what the measure does and does not establish and whether it is independent of the loop's own feedback signal.
- Primary source locations were inspected for design, outcome, and limitation claims. Search summaries were used as leads only. Nature version is linked to the published article; its within-paper experiments form one provenance cluster. Thakkar's Nature article and arXiv preprint are one ICLR 2025 trial. METR's early and late studies are distinct cohorts within one research program; the update is treated as author-reported qualification, not independent replication.
- Positive, null/mixed, and adverse evidence are represented conditionally. Missing evidence categories and the bounded search limit are stated rather than converted into null-effect claims.
- The evidence does not support a universal recommendation that more loops, more checkpoints, human oversight, or a fixed number of revisions improves agent workflows. The strongest cross-case inference is that continuation is more defensible when new feedback is relevant, discriminative, and externally checkable; otherwise extra revisions can alter a correct result, create cost, or reinforce a biased signal. That inference is grounded in E1, E3, E5, E6 and S2 E2, and remains untested as a single protocol across user workflows.
- **Stop rationale:** Worked the planned source roles through direct workflow cases in reviewed S1/S2, a new randomized live feedback/revision deployment, primary human–AI condition-manipulation experiments, independent benchmark refutations of self-correction, code-review repair/verification tests, and the contemporaneous update to the known productivity RCT. Followed paper references/versions and publisher pages. The main evidence distinctions now appear stable: reliable external signals can help in bounded tasks; poor/no signal and unverified self-correction can harm; human control and checkpointing do not ensure accuracy; objective quality, perceived usefulness, time and effort can diverge. Further search could add examples but is less likely to change these classifications. A fully current answer about optimal stopping, long-term resource cost, or a user's specific workflow still needs direct comparisons. This is an adequacy judgment, not exhaustive coverage.
